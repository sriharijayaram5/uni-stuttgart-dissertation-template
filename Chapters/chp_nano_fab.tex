\chapter{Nano- and microfabrication}\label{sec:fabrication}

This chapter outlines the fabrication processes central to this work, beginning with the motivation behind structuring diamond at the nanoscale. We provide a comparative analysis of current scanning probe architectures, highlighting the evolution from simple vertical pillars to parabolic geometries. We then introduce the structures developed in this thesis as an evolutionary step forward, designed to enhance sensitivity through the use an ensemble of NVs and while retaining the high spatial resolution required for scanning applications. Detailed discussions on design choices, mask materials, and specific microfabrication steps are presented to offer a complete view of the considerations in developing a scanning probe.

\section{Introduction}
Color centers in diamond is a leading and continuously developing platform for many quantum technologies in quantum information applications\cite{childressDiamondNVCenters2013, wrachtrupProcessingQuantumInformation2006} and advanced sensing\cite{taylorHighsensitivityDiamondMagnetometer2008, schirhaglNitrogenVacancyCentersDiamond2014}, by virtue of its excellent spin properties and optical addressability. The utility of the NV center and other color centers is fundamentally dependent on the properties of the host material, diamond. On one hand, diamond has many advantages such as a wide electronic bandgap of $\sim\qty{5.5}{\electronvolt}$, high thermal conductivity, mechanical robustness, chemical inertness, and bio-compatibility making it an attractive choice for many applications. Crystalline defects in diamond in some combination of displacement of substitution of native carbon exhibits many optically active defects, electronically isolated in the bulk band gap, and with control over spatial density, entering diamond in the realm of quantum optics. These defects also exhibit low phonon-induced relaxation due to the high Debye temperature of diamond. Furthermore, the availability of single crystal diamonds, with a relatively low natural abundance of nuclear spins leads to a magnetic noise free environment making it a perfect host for spins with long coherence times\cite{balasubramanianUltralongSpinCoherence2009}.

The conventional and convenient optical addressability of defects in diamond means efficiently collecting the emitted photons; due to a high refractive index of $\sim$2.4 and a resulting total internal reflection critical angle of $\sim\qty{25}{\degree}$\cite{zhuMulticoneDiamondWaveguides2023a}; is challenging. In this regard, diamond nanofabrication is an enabling development for many quantum technologies and thus emerges the field of diamond nanophotonics\cite{andoSmoothHighrateReactive2002, baynTriangularNanobeamPhotonic2011}.

\section{Nanophotonics and quantum sensing}

The field of quantum sensing has made great advances making use of the ultra long coherence times approaching a few \qty{}{ms}\cite{balasubramanianUltralongSpinCoherence2009} and magnetic mapping with nanometer spatial resolution\cite{appelFabricationAllDiamond2016} possible with NV centers in diamond. This has established magnetic field sensing with high sensitivity and high spatial resolution as one of the key applications of NV centers, even seeing commercial viability. An ideal magnetometer would be able to approach infinitesimally close to the magnetic field source and probe the magnetic signal for infinitely long, and convey this information via a measurement with an infinite signal-to-noise(SNR) ratio. To approach these ideals for a NV based magnetometer, it is desirable to employ near surface NV centers, to achieve proximity to the source; to retain good coherence properties of the NV center, to enable long probing times; and to fabricate a nanophotonic waveguide that enhances the collected fluorescence, increasing the sensitivity.

A suitable conclusion to these requirements is a diamond waveguide in the shape of a pillar with an NV center at the apex, which enables high photon collection and proximity to a source, with well controlled etching processes that preserve coherence. A simple pillar like nanowire geometry was shown by \textcite{babinecDiamondNanowireSinglephoton2010} to enhance photon flux by ten, presenting an outlook for a new class of devices for photonic and quantum information processing based on nanostructured diamond, and directly impacting scanning probe microscopy. 

\textcite{momenzadehNanoengineeredDiamondWaveguide2015} improved on this with a truncated cone geometry optimized by simulation and some control of etch chemistry a high net photon flux of $\sim\qty{1.7e6}{\second^{-1}}$, and a preserved $T_2^{\text{HE}}\sim\qty{23}{\micro\second}$. This is a significant step forward and enhances the sensitivity by a factor of $\sim 5$.

Further advances in diamond nanofabrication allows for a truncated diamond paraboloid having an NV at its focal point, and with an exit aperture designed to allow emission into a small numerical aperture\cite{hedrichParabolicDiamondScanning2020a}. This results in and increased photon flux of $\sim\qty{2.1e6}{\second^{-1}}$ at $P_{sat}\sim\qty{14}{\micro\watt}$ and improved proximity to the scanned sample by virtue of its parabolic geometry.

Though these approaches produce some of the brightest single photon solid state sources, there is room for improvement with regard to optimized collection efficiency, design-first approach, and process reliability and simplicity. \textcite{zhuMulticoneDiamondWaveguides2023a} develop a so-called multicone diamond waveguide that rely on a simple metal mask based etching recipe allowing for a high degree of control over the waveguide height. 

The previous approaches were limited by the choice of Hydrogen silsesquioxane (HSQ) based negative resists that act as a hard mask for diamond etching. The thickness as selectivity defined the maximum height achievable for the waveguides before the resists is depleted. The choice of metal mask for2 the waveguides enables more design freedom, and thus a height optimized for high collection efficiency. The multicone waveguides show emitted fluorescence is primarily into a low NA cone therefore reducing experimental cost and complexity associate with high-NA optics.

These works focus on the prolific NV center but is still mainly utilizing single NV based sensing. The reason to use a single center being spatial resolution need for nanoscale sensing, and sensitivity achievable for a scanning probe sensor. Let us consider the point of sensitivity. The practical figure of merit to consider is the pulsed ODMR sensitivity
\begin{equation}\label{eq:sens_shot}
    \eta_{\text{pulsed}} \approx \frac{8}{3\sqrt{3}} \frac{\hbar}{g_e \mu_b} \frac{1}{C_{\text{pulsed}}\sqrt{\mathscr{N}}} 
    \frac{\sqrt{t_I + T_2^* + t_R}}{T_2^*}.
\end{equation}
The numerical prefactors appear due to the lineshape of the pulsed ODMR curve, $g_e \approx 2.003$ is the NV center's electronic g factor\cite{dohertyNitrogenvacancyColourCentre2013}, $\mu_B$ is the Bohr magneton, $\hbar$ is the reduced Planck constant, $C_{\text{pulsed}}$ is the pulsed ODMR contrast, $T_2^*$ is the characteristic dephasing time for a Ramsey type measurement, $t_I$ is the initialization time and $t_R$ is the readout time. The time-averaged photon collection rate is $R$, then the mean number of photons collected per optical readout cycle is $\mathscr{N}=Rt_R$\cite{barrySensitivityOptimizationNVdiamond2020}. 

For a preserved $T_2^*$, it is desirable to preserve $C_{\text{pulsed}}$ while increasing $\mathscr{N}$. To do this the sensor number, $N$, density or interrogation volume can be increased. An increase of the interrogation volume is not conducive to the application of NV as high spatial resolution sensor. The sensitivity scales as $1/\sqrt{N}$ because of shot noise, which gives modest enhancement of $\sim5$ times\cite{barrySensitivityOptimizationNVdiamond2020}. 

Nevertheless, the increase of the number of defects increases yield of devices which typically rely on a probabilistic presence of implanted single defects within, for example, the apex of a probe waveguide. Shallow NV centers also experience charge state instabilities, sometimes irreversible, that can render entire probes unusable,\cite{neethirajanControlledSurfaceModification2023}. An increase in the number of defects allows for longer lifetimes of shallow NV devices, albeit with degraded performance. Shallow NV centers can also be incorporated by CVD growth in a $<111>$ orientation diamond substrate with preferential NV axis alignment\cite{michlPerfectAlignmentPreferential2014}. It is therefore desirable to have multiple NV centers in the use as scanning magnetic probe, if the second figure of merit can be retained.

The second figure of merit is spatial resolution of the magnetic probe. The NV center can achieve a spatial resolution as a sensor of the order of $\sim\qty{1}{\nano\meter}$, which is defined by the spatial extent of its electronic wavefunction\cite{appelFabricationAllDiamond2016}. In practice, the resolution is defined by the proximity of NV center to the sample, which under ideal contact condition of probe and sample, is limited by the depth of the NV center under the diamond surface. This has been controlled by tuning the implantation energy of the dopant nitrogen in the case of defects created by implantation\cite{santonocitoNVCentresVacancies2024}, or via CVD growth conditions\cite{osterkampEngineeringPreferentiallyalignedNitrogenvacancy2019}. 

The presence of multiple NV here presents the issue that the spatial resolution is diminished due to the random placement of the defect within the apex of the pillar, meaning a spread within a diameter of $\sim\qty{500}{\nano\meter}$. This loss of resolution cannot be mitigated but can be controlled by precise nanofabrication of the apex of the pillar such that the NV centers are localized in a well-defined volume. 

This chapter describes a method to create monolithic diamond quantum sensing probes that utilize an ensemble of preferentially aligned NV centers that are well localized at the apex of the diamond pillar.

\section{Diamond waveguides - design and fabrication}

The fabrication of diamond waveguides presents distinct engineering challenges driven by the cost, etch resistance, and fragility of the starting material, specifically, electronic-grade single-crystalline (type IIa) diamond membranes with thicknesses of $\sim\qty{30}{\micro\meter}$ and typical lateral dimensions of $\sim\qty{2}{\milli\meter}\times\qty{2}{\milli\meter}$\cite{torosReactiveIonEtching2020}. 

Given the investment in material preparation prior to nanofabrication, process reliability and yield are important. Diamond is a very suitable material for quantum sensing in harsh environments due to its chemical inertness. This very inertness also poses the largest challenge for its nanofabrication, since it requires a high density of inductively coupled plasma etching (ICP-RIE) with an oxygen chemistry to achieve a straight sidewall transfer of a design. Such harsh plasma imposes the requirement of high selectivity mask materials for high aspect ratio etching of diamond. One suitable option is to use metal mask deposited in a lift-off processes such as Cr, Al, Ti\cite{torosReactiveIonEtching2020}, although sidewall roughness is a concern.

Another commonly used process for the creation of diamond waveguides is to use a negative tone electron beam resist that closes resembles \ce{SiO2} on development, Hydrogen silsesquioxane (HSQ). HSQ based resists such as Flowable Oxide (FOx, DuPont) and Medusa 84 \ce{SiH} (Allresist) are versatile, provide low sidewall roughness, and enable grayscale lithography\cite{khoninaGrayscaleLithographyBrief2024,opaluchMedusa84SiH2025} while showing significant etch resistance to \ce{O2} plasma. 

On the other hand, HSQ based resists are significantly expensive and have poor shelf life. On expiration, HSQ first shows increased dose and the appearance of scum between features\cite{yangUnderstandingHydrogenSilsesquioxane2009,rooksHSQStorage} as shown in \cref{fig:hsq_scum}. This leads to unexpected failure of the process and increases the number of handling steps of the delicate diamond membranes. Furthermore, the thorough removal of developed HSQ is only possible with \ce{HF} acid or Buffered oxide etchant (BOE) that is dangerous to handle.

\section*{A PMMA only approach}

Alternative approaches to diamond waveguide fabrication exist, that do not use HSQ, such as etching using a silicon nitride hard mask. The process developed by \textcite{wanEfficientExtractionLight2018a, simonBuildingPlatformMagnetic2023}, uses the reflow of a cylinder of CSAR (positive tone e-beam resist), which takes on the shape of a cone. This shape is then transferred to a \ce{Si3N4} hard mask using a \ce{CHF3}/\ce{O2} etch. The \ce{Si3N4} then acts as hard mask for the diamond etch. This process offers improved taper angles and process reliability, making it a more desirable process than one HSQ based. 

The waveguide taper angle of $\sim\qty{13}{\degree}$ achieved in this process is defined by the fixed contact angle $\theta_c \approx \qty{14}{\degree}$ assumed by CSAR on reflow. Tuning of the waveguide height involves tuning of the thickness of resist and \ce{Si3N4} layers. Creation of the CSAR cylinders can also be challenging since it is a positive tone resist out of which the desired structure is a cylinder, resulting in a very long exposure with holes in the design mask. There might also be the need to do proximity dose correction to establish the correct doses for well-defined structures. Nevertheless, this is a suitable process to create diamond waveguides with reliability and reproducibility.

Another approach is selected as the basis for the waveguides developed here due to the simplicity of using the positive tone resist PMMA with metal lift-off hard mask\cite{zhuMulticoneDiamondWaveguides2023a}. PMMA (Polymethyl methacrylate) is a robust resist that can achieve high resolution e-beam lithography. It is very forgiving to dose variations and has a long shelf life. It also exhibits good adhesion to the diamond surface during spin coating without the need for adhesion promoters. 

PMMA is typically used in a lift-off process wherein the exposed region is removed during development. A metal mask is then deposited, which acts as a hard mask for etching. To achieve easy lift-off of the metal mask a two layer PMMA spin coating is performed with the lower molecular weight PMMA as the first layer and the higher molecular weight as the second layer. This results in the creation of an overhang after exposure providing a discontinuity in the deposited metal, aiding lift-off. TODO Details in appendix.

\subsection{A fabrication friendly waveguide}

A recipe to achieve in-house a similar sidewall angle control, with tall waveguides, while being fabrication friendly is the aim of this work. Fabrication friendliness can be understood with repeatability, resilience to errors (user and machine), and proximity to design goal as the criteria. These criteria are also kept in mind when deciding on the processes used here. 

Initial attempts of the PMMA approach are performed using an \ce{Al} hard mask. \ce{Al} deposition is difficult in the available e-beam evaporator for a large thickness (\qty{320}{\nano\meter}). \ce{Al} shows strong wetting behavior of the crucible and flows over onto the cooling adapter holding the crucible. For extended deposition times required for the large thickness, the \ce{Al} deposition is unpredictable in thickness, deposition quality, and cause large current fluctuations in the evaporator. Furthermore, \ce{Al} also oxidizes quickly forming an oxide layer that makes the initial heating of the metal for deposition unpredictable as well.

This low quality deposition leads large granularity in the mask. Over the course of the etching, this roughness is transferred to the diamond leading to rough structures. Due to these concerns, \ce{Al} is not further developed as a mask material for waveguides.

Titanium (\ce{Ti}), due its known high selectivity to diamond with \ce{O2} plasma, low selectivity with \ce{F} based plasma, and the availability in-house for e-beam evaporation is chosen for further development. \ce{Ti} also deposits granularly for large thicknesses, but it is observed that with the current plasma chemistry the \ce{Ti} undergoes smoothening of its walls. This leads to little transfer of roughness to the waveguide. \cref{tab:MC_etch_overview} shows the abstract etch recipe to fabricate a waveguide structure. The standard etch is used for a primarily straight etch that increases the height of the pillar and therefore adiabatic expansion of the mode. The secondary etch creates a slightly more tapered etch. The tertiary etch gives control over the taper angle, and top diameter of the waveguide. The waveguide is observed over the tertiary etch to determine the necessary number of repetitions. The duration of the individual steps in the tertiary etch can be tune to achieve various sidewall angles. 

For example, the diameter of the waveguide apex is measured using SEM after the secondary etch. The tertiary etch is performed with a few repetitions ($\sim6$) and the apex diameter is measured again, along with the height. This gives both vertical and lateral etch rates, allowing estimation of repetitions necessary to achieve the desired apex diameter. For the tertiary etch, typically, the vertical etch rate is $\sim\qty{160}{\nano\meter}/\text{repetition}$ and lateral etch rate is $\sim\qty{50}{\nano\meter}/\text{repetition}$. This granularity of etch rate of a single repetition is often sufficient, compared to varying the duration of steps. The total number of etch repetitions necessary can vary between samples due to variations in selectivity/thickness of the deposited metal mask, and spatial distribution of effective HF power in the RIE-ICP chamber\cite{chengImprovingFrontSide2012}. Further details are presented in \cref{app:metallization,app:metal_deposition}.


\cref{tab:MC_etch_chemistry} details the plasma chemistry used for the individual etching steps. It is noted that a \ce{CF4} based mask etch is more suitable than one \ce{SF6} based due to the lower and more controlled etch with \ce{CF4}. This may be due to protective polymerization due to the presence of free carbon atoms\cite{eiseleSF6Preferable1981} and the lower fluorine concentrations in the \ce{CF4} plasma\cite{tangTungstenEtchingCF1984}.

\subsection{Waveguide with nano-island}

The main objective of this chapter is to use these waveguides with multiple NV centers without largely compromising on the probe's spatial resolution. To achieve this, a $<111>$ orientation diamond substrate is used with a thin CVD growth layer of an ensemble of preferentially aligned NVs\cite{michlPerfectAlignmentPreferential2014}. 

The spatial extent of the NV centers and therefore the effective spatial resolution of the probe is controlled by thickness of the NV layer and the diameter of the apex of the waveguide. The thickness can be well controlled during growth and determined by confocal microscopy and SIMS (Secondary Ion Mass Spectroscopy), and can be reduced to a desired thickness after the growth by RIE-ICP etching of the surface uniformly.

Reducing the apex diameter of the waveguide leads to a reduced collection efficiency\cite{zhuMulticoneDiamondWaveguides2023a}. Instead, the apex diameter is left unchanged, and an additional etching step is included in the process. This step etches into the apex an additional cylinder (referred to as an island) which isolates the NV centers in given the island volume. 

FDTD (Finite-Difference Time Domain) simulation is performed to determine whether the collection efficiency is decreased by isolating the emitter in such a manner. \cref{fig:sim_all} represents the electric field intensity of the emission from a dipole placed \qty{50}{\nano\meter} below the end surface of the waveguide apex. The truncated cone waveguide shows directed emission, but into a higher NA. Both the parabolic waveguides, which are good approximations of the waveguides fabricated in this work, show emission into lower NA, increasing collection efficiency\cite{zhuMulticoneDiamondWaveguides2023a}. Notably, the island design still functions as a waveguide, and is suitable for use as a scanning probe.

The protruding island structure contains an NV ensemble, whereas the rest of the structure is for the purpose of waveguiding. 

\section{Waveguide on a cantilever}
\emph{Cantilever} (n.) \textipa{/"kantIli:v@/}: A long projecting beam or girder fixed at only one end, used chiefly in bridge construction—or, to host an NV center that probes the nanoscale properties of matter.

A complete diamond based scanning probe requires a freestanding waveguide that can come in proximity to a sample of interest. To achieve this, the diamond surrounding the waveguide is removed, with only a small bridge attaching the waveguide slab to a larger support structure. 

The process is split into two parts. The first part defines the shape of the cantilevers, attaching bridge, and support structure using a metal mask lift-off process. This mask is then etched into the diamond. The waveguide remains protected during this step by the same metal mask.

In part two, the diamond is flipped such that the waveguides face down and is then thinned down by etching. After etching through most of the diamond, the cantilevers are released since the surrounding diamond has been etched away in the previous step. 

At this stage, the structures are true cantilevers and can be broken of at the bridge, for attachment onto a suitable structure. The bridge is designed for likely fracture at the junction between the support structure and start of the bridge. See \cref{fig:bridge_snap} for a simulated structure.

\section*{Cantilever mask}

The mask for defining the cantilever is created with a process similar to the previous sections. A two layer PMMA resist is used along with \qty{250}{\nano\meter} of \ce{Cr} as the hard mask. \ce{Cr} is chosen as the hard mask due to high selectivity with diamond under \ce{O2} plasma, and ease of removal via wet chemical etching. 

The cantilever is designed to be large enough for easy detachment and pick-up, small enough to have a high density of devices, and with a bridge structure that can be easily snapped. Since the support structures and cantilevers are relatively large scale structures ($\sim\qty{5}{\micro\meter}$), with significant proximity e-beam exposure, the dose for the smaller scale ($\sim\qty{100}{\nano\meter}$) bridge structure can be too low. To account for this proximity effect correction is performed to make an additional dose factor map for the bridge structure, ensuring reliable exposure. Nevertheless, PMMA is very forgiving in this regard, and transfers the structure over a large range of doses. On the other hand, negative tone resist Medusa, shows incorrect exposure without proximity effect correction (\cref{fig:medusa_cantiA}).

\section*{Conclusion and outlook}
Scanning probe microscopy using NV centers in diamond require reliable and accessible probes. Key to this is a high yield, cost-effective, tunable, and reproducible process of creating waveguides. The use of an ensemble of preferentially aligned NV confined to a known volume promises such a device. This is established in this chapter with the introduction of island-waveguides that are fabricated using \ce{Ti} mask and a tailored RIE-ICP process. The cantilevers are also developed in a similar process to deliver high yield.

Validation of these probes as high spatial resolution and sensitive magnetometers requires further assembly of cantilevers with the carrier chip.
