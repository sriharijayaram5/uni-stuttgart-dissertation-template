\section{Experimental setup and tools}

\subsection{Magnetic field alignment and hysterisis}
The majority of this work is performed on a AttoDRY 2200 cryostat (Attocube) with a \qty{1}{\tesla} vector magnet with a \qty{9}{\tesla} coil for the $z$-axis. To explore interesting phases of material an external magnetic field is applied to the materials, for example in a field cooldown procedure. For small magnetic fields ($\lesssim\qty{10}{\milli\tesla}$), the alignment of the external field to the NV center axis is not critical, but for larger fields, especially near the ground state and excited state level anti-crossings, the NV fluorescence is greatly quenched due to spin mixing. The mechanics of this covered deeply by \citeauthor{tetienneMagneticfielddependentPhotodynamicsSingle2012}\cite{tetienneMagneticfielddependentPhotodynamicsSingle2012}. This effect can in fact be used for qualitative mapping of magnetic field, but in many instances necessitate a well aligned magnetic field. The magnetic field alignment can be done in steps of increasing precision, but the precision of alignment is always limited by the accuracy of the applied field. This is a challenge due to the superconducting magnetics that tend to retain a remnant field. An automated protocol is used here to remove a majority of the remnant field. A field alignment is then performed using the NV fluorescence and then using a series of ODMR measurement with progressive correction.

The alignment process begins with removal of the remnant magnetic field. The hysteresis effect is most noticeable after working at higher fields ($\gtrsim\qty{75}{\milli\tesla}$) and then returning to set zero field by simply ramping down the individual axis, leaving a remnant field of several \unit{\milli\tesla}.

For this particular system with its magnet configuration, and vectors fields applied typically not exceeding \qty{200}{\milli\tesla} the following protocol is used and greatly reduces the remnant field. Irrespective of the starting conditions, the magnet is first ramped down to zero field. An axis individually ramped to a starting value of \qty{50}{\milli\tesla}. The field is then reversed in direction along the axis. This training of the field is what reduces the remnant field. This procedure is repeated in 20 steps with decreasing field values until zero field. The procedure is then performed on the other axis. The training procedure can also be done along any arbitrary axis if necessary and the commands for both the procedure are available in the 'magnetic field tools' notebook\cite{jayaramQudiScanningProbe2026}.

Now the field alignment using the NV fluorescence is performed. This procedure is available with a GUI in 'magnet control' module\cite{jayaramQudiScanningProbe2026}. Depending on the known crystallographic orientation of the diamond probe used, the magnetic field sweep range is set. A fixed vector field magnitude $\rho$ is used while the in plane angle $\phi$ and the azimuthal angle $\theta$ is swept. The NV fluorescence is averaged at each point and maximum in fluorescence is seen when the field is well aligned\cite{tetienneMagneticfielddependentPhotodynamicsSingle2012}.

If a better aligned field is required, for example at high magnetic field or close to a LAC, then field alignment using the ODMR method is performed. This is detailed in a notebook titled 'magnetic field alignment - ODMR method'\cite{jayaramQudiScanningProbe2026}. The method is based on the work from\citeauthor{thielQuantitativeNanoscaleVortex2016}\cite{thielQuantitativeNanoscaleVortex2016}. An illustration of this method is shown in \cref{fig:alignA}. 

This method is used to find the two unknowns of $\theta_0$, and $\phi_0$, which are the spherical coordinates defined w.r.t to the NV axis and $\mathbf{B}_{\text{init}}$. The method requires precise control over the external magnetic field and is therefore suitable for a vector magnet. It is also based on the fact that the magnetic fields applied parallel to the NV axis couple to the NV spin and the perpendicular fields only introduce a weak, second order effect. For a field parallel to the NV axis, the ODMR resonance frequency is shifted by $\Delta\nu_{\text{ESR}} = \gamma_{\text{NV}}\lvert \mathbf{B}_{\text{init}} \rvert$ with $\gamma_{\text{NV}}=\qty{28}{\mega\hertz/\milli\tesla}$. For a weak perpendicular field $\mathbf{B}_{\text{perp}}$ in addition to this, no change in the ODMR position is seen irrespective of angle $\alpha$ (in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf{B}_{\text{init}}$), but only when $\mathbf{B}_{\text{init}}$ is perfectly along the NV axis. This serves as a check for magnetic field alignment.

The magnetic field direction found from the fluorescence measurement or best last known direction is used to define the $\mathbf{B}_{\text{init}}$. 
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{B}_{\text{init}} = B_{\text{init}} 
\begin{pmatrix}
\sin(\theta_0) \cos(\phi_0) \\
\sin(\theta_0) \sin(\phi_0) \\
\cos(\theta_0)
\end{pmatrix}
\end{equation}

From here a set of $\mathbf{B}_{\text{perp}}$ are determined for a list of $\alpha$. This gives a set of 
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{B}(\alpha) = B_{\text{init}} \hat{\mathbf{e}}_i + R_{\mathbf{B}_{\text{init}}}(\alpha) B_{\text{perp}} \hat{\mathbf{e}}_p
\end{equation}
where $R_{\mathbf{B}_{\text{init}}}(\alpha)$ is the rotation matrix by angle $\alpha$ around $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_i$, and $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{i(p)}$ is the unit vector along $\mathbf{B}_{\text{init}(\text{perp})}$.

An ODMR measurement is then performed for over all $\mathbf{B}(\alpha)$. For a misaligned field this gives a wandering ODMR spectrum as shown in \cref{fig:alignA}. For an angle $\alpha$ we then have 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
       \Delta\nu_{\text{ESR}}(\alpha) &= \gamma_{\text{NV}} \hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\text{NV}} \cdot \mathbf{B}(\alpha) \\
       &= \gamma_{\text{NV}} \left[ B_{\text{init}} \cos(\theta_0) - B_{\text{perp}} \sin(\theta_0) \sin(\alpha) \right]. 
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

The quantity $\Delta\nu_{\text{ESR}}$ is then determined from the fit of the ODMR resonance positions that show a sinusoidal behavior over $\alpha$. The  $\mathbf{B}_{\text{perp}}$ for maximum splitting is also determined. This is used to determine the $\theta_0$ as
\begin{equation}
    \theta_0 = sin^{-1} \left( \frac{\Delta\nu_{\text{ESR}}}{2 \gamma_{\text{NV}} B_{\text{perp}}} \right).
\end{equation}

The rotation axis for $\theta_0$ is known from the cross product between $\mathbf{B}_{\text{perp}}$ and $\mathbf{B}_{\text{init}}$. Then the first estimate of the NV axis is determined by rotation of the $\mathbf{B}_{\text{init}}$ in the cross product plane by $\theta_0$ amount. This procedure iteratively gives better values for $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\text{NV}}$.

An additional alignment procedure can be performed if the resulting field orientations do not converge. This assumes that since the $\theta_0$ is very sensitive to the set values for $\mathbf{B}_{\text{perp}}$ being accurate, that the $\theta_0$ may be wrong in value, but is nevertheless in the correct plane. In this case, the procedure would benefit from a sweep of $\theta_0$ now in the plane of $\hat{\mathbf{e}}_{\text{NV}}$ and $\mathbf{B}_{\text{init}}$, previously determined. This is also iteratively done, as well as for the orthogonal plane, which yields good magnetic field alignment.

\subsection{Gradiometric fluorescence readout}\label{app:grad_pl_readout}
A technical detail that is important to the realization of all measurements with synchronization of the mechanical oscillation with readout (gradiometric), is the choice of analysis method for the readout. Typically, a normalized mean over the many readout repetitions is performed. The signal window ($\sim\qty{200}{\nano\second}$) is at the beginning of the readout and gives the spin contrast and the reference window of the same width is to the tail of the readout once the steady state population is achieved. The signal is normalized to this reference (mean-norm method). In the case of gradiometric readout, since the fluorescence varies depending on the position of the emitter under the excitation beam (see \cref{fig:pl_osc}), the mean-norm method is invalid. The analysis is therefore only done with a summation over the signal window.

\subsection{Maxwell's equations}\label{app:Maxwell}
The four Maxwell’s equations are expressed as:

\begin{align}
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{D} &= \rho_e  \\
\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} &= 0  \\
\nabla \times \mathbf{E} + \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t} &= 0  \\
\nabla \times \mathbf{H} - \frac{\partial \mathbf{D}}{\partial t} &= \mathbf{j} ,
\end{align}

where $\rho_e$ is the charge density and $\mathbf{j}$ is the electric current density. The displacement field in SI units is defined as $\mathbf{D} = \epsilon \epsilon_0 \mathbf{E}$, and the magnetic flux density is related to the magnetic field intensity by $\mathbf{B} = \mu \mu_0 \mathbf{H}$ for permittivity $\epsilon$ and permeability $\mu$.

\subsection{Cantilever bridge simulation}

The cantilever bridge attaching the cantilever to the support structure is simulated. The motivation is to determine a better shape or breaking action of the cantilever such that snapping it off the support structure is easier. It is known from previous efforts that even simple rectangular shaped bridge with a width of \qty{200}{\nano\meter} can prove challenging to break off, even capable of lifting the entire membrane with it. 

Since a new fabrication process is being development it is determined to be beneficial to consider other bridge geometries that aid the process. This is inspired by the concept of notch sensitivity. It is a material property measuring susceptibility to fatigue failure caused by stress-raising notches, holes, or defects\cite{ShigleysMechanicalEngineering,Notch_sensitivity}. A sharp right angle bend would act like a stress concentrator and therefore the cantilever side of the bridge has a smooth transition to the thin structure. At the joint with the support, the bridge structure is made to attach at a right angle. To verify this a COMSOL based stress simulation is performed as shown in \cref{fig:bridge_snap}. The dimensions of the structures simulated are in line with what is designed for and achievable in nanofabrication. 

Two simulations are performed with differing direction of applied force to simulate a glue and pick up vertically away from the membrane, and to move away in plane. It is seen that the moving in plane to snap the bridge is suitable due on order of magnitude more stress. The simulations confirm the stress concentration at the support side of the bridge due to notch sensitivity informed design, and it is determined that an in-plane snapping motion is more suitable.

\subsection{Cantilever creation with Medusa}
Cantilever hard masks are created with Medusa to determine suitability in the probe fabrication process. The bridge structure and its relatively smaller dimensions results in the insufficient dose. This is because the larger structures are exposed also from the neighboring write field due to proximity effect. The design exposed with uniform dose overall is shown in \cref{fig:medusa_cantiAa}. Note the missing bridge structure, as the design width narrows. 

To compensate for this a proximity effect correction is necessary such that a uniform thickness of the resist is present in all exposed regions as designed. This can be performed by simulation of the electron beam exposure trajectory knowing a few resist behavior parameters\cite{opaluchMedusa84SiH2025}. The Voyager (Raith Systems) software offers a module called nano-PECS which performs this simulation for a given design. The design is then broken up into smaller exposure blocks that are assigned individual dose factors, to account for the proximity effect. Note that this is computationally intensive during the simulation as well as during patterning, with an increase of $5\times$ of patterning times seen. 

Much of the pattern can sometimes have very small dose factor changes, as well as changes where nano-PECS is not necessary. The initial design could then be done in parts to reduce the overhead. Another workaround used is to simply copy parts of the output nano-PECS design which are critical and overlay it with the initial design. This achieves a similar result but with much shorter patterning times. This approach is used for patterning using PMMA (see \cref{fig:pmma_canti}). \cref{fig:medusa_cantiAb} shows patterning done with a fully nano-PECS design. Note the patterns and heigh variation in the in interior of the structure. This is due to imperfect estimation of nano-PECS parameters along with variation in resist sensitivity.

Instead of nano-PECS, a so-called poor man's PECS can be performed. This is simply done by overlaying a copy of part of the initial design, essentially locally increasing the dose factors where a lack of sufficient dose is expected or seen. This can be beneficial for a few reasons. There is no need for PECS parameter estimation, simulation and write time overhead, and results that are intuitive. The dose factors can be found using a dose sweep. \cref{fig:medusa_cantiAc} shows a bridge patterned using poor man's PECS. Note the lack of an internal pattern in the bulk of the cantilever due to the absence of PECS. The bridge is created but nevertheless suffers from height variations that is difficult to avoid in this approach. \cref{fig:medusa_cantiAd} shows a true nano-PECS design that is pattern. A smoother bridge is created but also suffers from reduced thickness due to improper parameters. This can prove problematic since Medusa is a hard mask that is directly used for etching. The thinner regions would be etched away before the thicker and have results that resemble inadvertent grayscale lithography, transferring these height features onto the diamond. 

Something else to note that is particular to Medusa is that there is also visible scumming\cite{rooksHSQStorage}. This is attributed to the short lifetime of HSQ based resists and due to storage conditions. This is hard to control in a shared user facility and with expensive resists. Also, there is the poor adhesion of the Medusa to diamond, although an adhesion promoter is used (SurPass 3000, DisChem). This is also attributed to degradation in storage, although this is at the early stages.

\subsection{Back side metallization}\label{app:metallization}

Although serendipitously found, back side metallization has greatly improved process uniformity and repeatability. This technique is used to create the sample holder that go into the RIE-ICP etching machine (Oxford PlasmaPro 100). The machine has an ICP column about \qty{4}{in} in diameter and the samples is placed on a larger graphite plate with active cooling. Various process gases such \ce{O2}, \ce{Ar}, \ce{SF6}, and \ce{CF4} are available. The samples are either directly placed on the graphite plate or in case of lighter smaller samples, using a carrier chip that is suitable with the etch chemistry. According to accumulated machine experience it is understood that samples smaller than the ICP column will result spatially uniform etches, although it is unclear whether this conclusion was ever rigorously investigated. 

On performing deep etches of $\sim\qty{25}{\micro\meter}$ in diamond ($\qty{2}{\milli\meter}\times\qty{2}{\milli\meter}$) on a sapphire carrier (\qty{4}{\milli\meter} diameter)  using an \ce{Ar}/\ce{SF6} etch chemistry, it is seen that etch height variations of up to $\sim\qty{10}{\micro\meter}$ are present. This results a complete loss of the sample and makes the machine unusable for such processes. It is unclear whether a particular combination of parameters aggravates this effect as compared to other processes since issues with deep etching, although not routinely performed, is not commonly heard of.

A similar effect is noticed when an \ce{Al} metal etch stop layer was removed from the process of a \ce{SiN} microstructure. The \ce{SiN} etch with the \ce{Al} underneath was homogenous for the entirety of the \qty{4}{in} wafer compared to without, where only a small region in the center of $\sim\qty{2}{\milli\meter}$ was seemingly etched. This is not further investigated and the \ce{Al} reintroduced to the process and the effect was attributed to temperature distribution over the wafer. Inspired by this seemingly unconnected effect, a simple test is performed to ascertain the etching behavior, especially pertaining to the spatial homogeneity of the etch, with and without a metal layer underneath. The results are shown in \cref{fig:back_metal}.

The test is performed by performing a similar etch of \ce{Ar}/\ce{SF6} chemistry on a \qty{4}{in} \ce{Si} wafer. Parts of the wafer are protected by additional small \ce{Si} chips placed along the diameter of the sample, allowing for etch height estimation. \cref{fig:back_metala} shows an optical image of the etch results on a wafer coated on the back side with \qty{50}{\nano\meter} of \ce{Cr} with e-beam evaporation. The etch appears very uniform optically as well under profilometry. \cref{fig:back_metalb} shows the results of a similar etch but without the back metallization. The difference is stark, showing rough etch pits away from the center and inhomogeneous etch rate. The test is repeated and yield similar results. There is not much literature on this effect other than a report by \citeauthor{chengImprovingFrontSide2012} where the similar effect is investigated in Plasma Enhanced-CVD reactors, with similar findings. The understanding is that the metal enhances the RF power coupling in the RIE-ICP chamber and distributes it evenly across the sample, which is otherwise a weak coupling for insulating samples.

As a result of this all etch processes in the RIE-ICP performed in this work are done using a \qty{50}{\milli\meter} diameter Sapphire carrier with \qty{50}{\nano\meter} of \ce{Cr} back coating. The metal choice is not detrimental to the process. The metal survives for many etch cycles and the Sapphire is chemically inert to the process gases. A deep etch performed on diamond using this process yields good results with only the initial non-planarity of the diamond causing height variations.

An inadvertent advantage of this process is also noted. The use of the large Sapphire carrier ensures that that carrier itself is laying parallel to the graphite etching plate, especially if the graphite plate has always been used in combination with the carrier. This means that the samples placed on the Sapphire carrier are significantly more parallel to the so-called etching plane, as opposed to when placed directly on to the graphite. Since the Sapphire is also robust against all the etching chemistry used here it also retains this flatness. The RIE-ICP machine is equipped with an endpoint detection system that uses a light camera with a very long working distance objective for a navigation and a \qty{657}{\nano\meter} laser for interferometric detection of etch rates. This system is unfortunately extremely sensitive to the angle of the sample w.r.t to the etching plane which is perpendicular to the beam path (with the possibility of some adjustment). Typically, samples placed directly on the graphite plate lead to irrecoverable tilted samples for endpoint detection. The sapphire carrier solves this problem as well.

\subsection[Metal deposition]{Metal deposition - selectivity and effective deposition rate}\label{app:metal_deposition}

Central to the nanofabrication in this work is the metal deposition process. The technique of e-beam evaporation with the Univex 400 (Leybold) is used for deposition in the shared user facility. A variety of metals are available for deposition at deposition rates of \qty{0.5}{\angstrom/\second} to \qty{2}{\angstrom/\second}. A few figures of merit determine the suitability of the deposited metal to the process. The initial choice of metal is the most important since it determines the suitability to the process. The surface roughness, sidewall roughness and quality of the metal is important for application where low resistance contact with material is to be created, or for the purpose of use as a hard etch mask and the transfer of a rough sidewall is undesirable. Certain metals deposit rougher than other for the deposition conditions, which is inherent to the technique and material properties. This could be addressed but require much time and effort, and may not be intrinsically suitable to the deposition technique. For example, \ce{Al} proves to be a troublesome metal to deposit. It quickly forms an oxide layer while stored in the crucible. This layer is difficult and unpredictable to melt and results initial high current in the evaporator which can be hard for the control loop to adjust to when reaching the metal. The \ce{Al} also exhibits different wetting behavior of the crucible, often riding up the walls and causing an overflow. This changes the equilibrium of the heat exchange between the crucible and the cooling jacket, also disrupting the control loop. The end results of such a deposition may be incorrect deposition thickness and degraded quality of metal. This can be verified by SEM imaging and by profilometry.

The etch selectivity of the metal to the process chemistry is also important, to achieve the necessary etch depth while retaining the metal mask. This selectivity is dependent on the deposition rate used. Literature on this is sparse and is only understood from collective experience. The effects of this can be quite drastic. For example, in an isolated test it is seen that the \ce{Ti} deposited with \qty{2}{\angstrom/\second} produced an etch resistance to \ce{O2} chemistry that was $10\times$ lower than to \ce{Ti} deposited with \qty{1}{\angstrom/\second}.

An important point to note is regarding the measurement of the tooling factor, $TF = \frac{T_{\text{actual}}}{T_{\text{crystal}}} \times TF_{\text{previous}}$, where $T_{\text{actual}}$ is the profilometry measured thickness and $T_{\text{crystal}}$ the thickness estimated by an oscillating piezo crystal in the chamber. This quantity is critical in compensating for the discrepancy that arises in the crystal measured thickness that is affected by factors such as deposition chamber size and shape, position of the crystal w.r.t the sample, crystal and chamber temperature, pressure, etc. Issues can arise when the $TF$ is calibrated only for thin depositions, resulting in incorrect deposition at thicker depositions. This maybe because of an accumulation of errors that are only measurable at higher thickness or due to a stronger and longer impact of factors such as temperature on the crystal. For a thickness such as \qty{300}{\nano\meter}, which is in the range of thicknesses used in this work, for the improperly calibrated $TF$ the deposited thickness can be as high as \qty{360}{\nano\meter}.\footnotemark\footnotetext{The extensive measurments and calibrations are performed by Iuliia Krämer (Cleanroom Process Engineer)}This is readily corrected by recalibrating the $TF$. As an additional measure, glass cover slides with cover slips as deposition masks are inserted along with the sample to be coated (placed not too far away from the sample). The slides are used to do control measurements to ensure that the deposited thickness is with in requirements.
