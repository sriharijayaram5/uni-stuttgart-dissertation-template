This chapter first provides an introduction to the London description of superconductivity as well as the Pearl model. These descriptions provide the necessary quantities to understand superconductivity of thin materials. Then \gls{SQM} is introduced as suitable probe of superconductivity in 2D materials due to its non perturbative probing, high sensitivity, and spatial resolution. The experimental results on \ce{NbSe2} are presented. The presence of superconductivity in a few layers and the vortex arrangement is mapped, revealing a disordered vortex glass phase. Further, cooling rate dependant vortex arrangement is studied with the presence of vortex melting phase and its role in vortex de-pinning noted. The \gls{NV} center with its dynamic sensing capabilities is used to probe the noise signatures of superconductivity. Unexpected results from \gls{SQM} spin coherence based noise measurements as function of sample-sensor distance and temperature is discussed, showing noise persisting well below the critical temperature. The layered nature of the studied superconductor provides interesting geometric features such as layer step junctions, which are rarely investigated. The vortex arrangement and shape in and around these step junctions are presented, revealing possible non-integer flux quantization.

\section{Superconductivity}

\subsection*{London description of superconductivity\footnotemark}
\footnotetext{The derivations follow notes by Alexander Tsirlin, Leipzig University\cite{TopicsQuantumMagnetism}}
Developed by the London brothers in 1935\cite{londonElectromagneticEquationsSupraconductor1935} this description is the first yet relevant today phenomenological theory of superconductivity. It is described using Maxwell's electrodynamics(\ref{app:Maxwell}) and the two postulates of superconductivity:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \text{Ideal conductor}: the state with $\rho = 0$.
    \item \text{Ideal diamagnet}: the state with $\mathbf{B} = 0$.
\end{itemize}

The dynamics of a charge carrier with mass $m_s$ and charge $e_s$ are governed by the equation of motion:
\begin{equation}
    m_s \frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt} = e_s \mathbf{E}
\end{equation}

For a condensate composed of Cooper pairs, these parameters are defined as $m_s = 2m_e$ and $e_s = -2e$. Defining the superfluid density $n_s$ as the concentration of electrons participating in the superconducting state, the density of charge carriers (pairs) is $n_s/2$. The resulting supercurrent density is expressed as $\mathbf{j}_s = -n_s e \mathbf{v}$. Substituting into the equation of motion yields the first London Equation:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:first_london}
    \frac{d\mathbf{j}_s}{dt} = \frac{n_s e^2}{m_e} \mathbf{E}
\end{equation}

This expression describes the temporal variation of the supercurrent in response to a local electric field. Comparing this with the standard Drude model for DC conductivity:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{j} = \frac{n_e e^2 \tau}{m_e} \mathbf{E}
\end{equation}

While the forms are mathematically similar, the First London equation depicts a scattering-free limit. For an applied electric field the charge carriers undergo constant acceleration, and retain their velocity of zero field, unlike a normal conductor.

By taking the curl of \cref{eq:first_london} and $\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$, we obtain:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \left( \nabla \times \mathbf{j}_s + \frac{n_s e^2}{m_e} \mathbf{B} \right) = 0.
\end{equation}

Integrating this and setting the integration constant to zero (corresponding to the Meissner effect) yields the second London equation:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:second_london}
    \nabla \times \mathbf{j}_s = -\frac{n_s e^2}{m_e} \mathbf{B}
\end{equation}

This relation defines the spatial variation of the supercurrent and leads directly magnetic flux expulsion within the bulk of a superconductor. \cref{eq:first_london,eq:second_london} may be expressed in terms of the magnetic vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ as a single London equation:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{j}_s = -\frac{n_s e^2}{m_e} \mathbf{A}
\end{equation}

where $\mathbf{A}$ is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{B} = \nabla \times \mathbf{A}, \quad \mathbf{E} = -\nabla \phi - \frac{\partial \mathbf{A}}{\partial t}
\end{equation}

In this representation, $\phi$ denotes the scalar electrostatic potential. The London equations are note gauge-invariant and a London gauge\footnotemark  is required to satisfy this transformation.
\footnotetext{$\nabla \phi=0$; $\nabla\cdot\mathbf{A}=0$ (from charge conservation - Coulomb gauge)}

\subsection*{Penetration depth}

The spatial distribution of the magnetic field and supercurrent within a superconductor is determined by decoupling the London equations using standard vector identities. In a stationary state, Ampere’s Law is given by $\nabla \times \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 \mathbf{j}_s$. Applying the curl operator to both sides:

\begin{equation}
    \nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) = \mu_0 (\nabla \times \mathbf{j}_s)
\end{equation}

Utilizing the vector identity $\nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{B}) = \nabla(\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B}) - \nabla^2 \mathbf{B}$, and noting that $\nabla \cdot \mathbf{B} = 0$ as per Gauss’s Law, we arrive at:

\begin{equation}
    -\nabla^2 \mathbf{B} = \mu_0 (\nabla \times \mathbf{j}_s)
\end{equation}

Substituting \cref{eq:second_london}, we obtain:

\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2 \mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0 n_s e^2}{m_e} \mathbf{B} = \frac{\mathbf{B}}{\lambda_L^2}
\end{equation}

The characteristic length scale is the London penetration depth $\lambda_L$, which is defined as:

\begin{equation}
    \lambda_L = \sqrt{\frac{m_e}{\mu_0 n_s e^2}}
\end{equation}

This parameter quantifies the extent to which a magnetic field penetrates the superconducting bulk. The exponential decay solution, $\mathbf{B}(x) = \mathbf{B}(0) e^{-x/\lambda_L}$, describes the Meissner Effect, where $x>0$ is inside the superconductor.

An analogous equation describes the spatial distribution of the supercurrent density $\mathbf{j}_s$:

\begin{equation}
    \nabla^2 \mathbf{j}_s = \frac{\mathbf{j}_s}{\lambda_L^2}
\end{equation}

This describes that both the magnetic field and the screening currents responsible for magnetic field expulsion are confined to a thin surface layer of thickness $\lambda_L$.

\subsection*{Generalized London equation}
The charge carriers in the superconductor can be described by a macroscopic wavefunction (order parameter) of the superconducting state
\begin{equation}
    \Psi(\mathbf{r}) = \sqrt{n_s/2} e^{-i\theta(\mathbf{r})},
\end{equation}

where $\theta(\mathbf{r})$ denotes the phase, and the amplitude $\sqrt{n_s/2}$ ensures the normalization of $|\Psi(\mathbf{r})|^2$ to the charge carrier concentration. This $\theta(\mathbf{r})$ is the singular phase assigned to the condensate.

The momentum operator $\hat{\mathbf{p}} = -i\hbar\nabla$ acting upon the macroscopic wavefunction gives:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{p} = \frac{\langle \Psi | \hat{\mathbf{p}} | \Psi \rangle}{\langle \Psi | \Psi \rangle} = -\hbar \nabla \theta
\end{equation}

In the presence of a magnetic field, this gives the canonical momentum of a Cooper pair:

\begin{equation}
    \mathbf{p} = 2m_e \mathbf{v} - 2e \mathbf{A} \tag{3.19}
\end{equation}

This gives both the kinetic momentum and the magnetic potential. This gives the derivation of the supercurrent density $\mathbf{j}_s = -n_s e \mathbf{v}$:

\begin{align}
    \mathbf{j}_s &= \frac{n_s e}{m_e} \left( \frac{\hbar}{2} \nabla \theta - e \mathbf{A} \right) \\
    &= \frac{1}{\mu_0 \lambda_L^2} \left( \frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi} \nabla \theta - \mathbf{A} \right),
\end{align}

where $\Phi_0 = \frac{\pi\hbar}{e}$ represents the magnetic flux quantum. This is called the generalized London equation. This result illustrates that the supercurrent is driven by both the gradient of the superconducting phase and the external magnetic field.

\subsection*{Flux quantization}

The generalized London equation directly leads to flux quantization. Consider a superconducting loop in an external magnetic field, with an integration path in this loop far from the boundary such that $\mathbf{j}_s = 0$. The path integral is also then zero giving:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi} \oint \nabla \theta \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l}
\end{equation}

Per Stokes' theorem, the closed-loop line integral of the vector potential $\mathbf{A}$ is equivalent to the surface integral of its curl, $\nabla \times \mathbf{A} = \mathbf{B}$. The right-hand side thus represents the total magnetic flux $\Phi$ threading the loop:
\begin{equation}
    \oint \mathbf{A} \cdot d\mathbf{l} = \iint \mathbf{B} \cdot d\mathbf{S} = \Phi
\end{equation}

This shows the relation between the line integral over $\nabla \theta$ and $\Phi$. Integrating over the gradient of the phase over a full loop gives a change proportional to $2\pi$. We have then
\begin{equation}
    \Phi = \frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi} \cdot 2\pi n = n\Phi_0
\end{equation}
where $n \in \mathbb{Z}$. This result reveals a fundamental property of superconductors: the magnetic flux enclosed in a superconducting loop is constrained to discrete values. The magnetic flux quantum, $\Phi_0$, is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_0 = \frac{2\pi\hbar}{|e_s|} = \frac{h}{2e} = \qty{2.0678e-15}{\tesla\meter^2}
\end{equation}

\subsection*{Pearl length}
In 1964, Pearl\cite{pearlCURRENTDISTRIBUTIONSUPERCONDUCTING1964} presents a different behaviour of superconductivity and vortex profiles in thin superconductors. It is suggested that the spatial extent of magnetic field screening is in this case governed instead by the Pearl length 
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda = \frac{2\lambda_L^2}{d}, 
\end{equation}
whee $\lambda_L$ is the London penetration depth.

This description is based on the London theory and therefore assumes a constant superfluid density and a point-like vortex core. It introduces a long range interaction force in the superconductor, and reduced field screening, unlike in bulk excitations\cite{pearlCURRENTDISTRIBUTIONSUPERCONDUCTING1964}. This is not a complete description however, due to the assumption of the London theory. The \gls{GL} theory of superconductors, a phenomenological theory, introduces again penetration depth and coherence length $\eta$ for the order parameter $\psi$. This is identical to the London formalism scaled by dependence on the order parameter, accounting for the local change in charge carriers. The coherence length defines the length scale over which the superconducting order parameter scales spatially, and therefore also the size of the vortex core. This then gives the \gls{GL} parameter $\kappa=\lambda/\eta$. For superconductors where the parameter $\kappa\gg1$ is the case where London theory holds\cite{balzliRealisticPearlVortices2026}. Departing this regime gives us non-negligible vortex core sizes, and makes the description of thin superconductors non-trivial. This makes the nanometre scale mapping of these profiles relevant.

While the London and \gls{GL} frameworks provide phenomenological macroscopic descriptions, the microscopic mechanism of superconductivity is delineated by \gls{BCS} theory\cite{bardeenTheorySuperconductivity1957}. 

\gls{BCS} theory postulates that superconductivity emerges when the attractive interaction between electrons, mediated by virtual phonon exchange, overcoming the repulsive screened Coulomb interaction. This results in a ground state composed of correlated electron pairs with opposite spin and momentum, or a condensate of pairs called Cooper pairs. The theory predicts and aligns with the following properties of superconductors such as the Meissner effect, the existence of a superconducting gap, the superconducting isotope effect, and the second order superconducting phase transition. The superconductors that can be well explained by the \gls{BCS} theory are called conventional superconductors, and the systems that go beyond this theory are called unconventional superconductors.

\section{\ce{NbSe2}}

In 1964, \citeauthor{ginzburgTwodimensionalSuperconductors1989} discusses the problem of two-dimensional superconductivity\cite{ginzburgTwodimensionalSuperconductors1989}. A phase transition in one-dimensional systems is understood to be impossible at a non-zero temperature\cite{merminAbsenceFerromagnetismAntiferromagnetism1966}, but superconductivity of surfaces is seemingly possible as demonstrated in many early experiments. This is understood to be due to the emergence of a quasi-long range order. One of the earliest works demonstrating such superconductivity was on \ce{NbSe2} down to two atomic layers\cite{frindtSuperconductivityUltrathinNb1972}. Interestingly, this work also shows an early demonstration of 2D material exfoliation. Since then, the material has been the subject of several studies in the bulk and few layer limit and is considered a prototypical layered transition metal dichalcogenide. While bulk 2H-\ce{NbSe2} is a well-studied type-II anisotropic multiband superconductor with a $T_c$ of approximately \qty{7}{\kelvin}, the transition to the 2D limit introduces unique phenomena.

A primary motivation for studying monolayer \ce{NbSe2} is the presence of strong intrinsic spin-orbit interactions combined with a broken in-plane inversion symmetry. In the monolayer limit, the crystal structure lacks a center of inversion, which, when coupled with the 4d transition metal's heavy atoms, generates a large effective magnetic field. This field locks the electron spins to the out-of-plane direction\cite{xuSpinPseudospinsLayered2014}. This phenomenon, known as Ising pairing, protects the superconducting state against external magnetic fields that would typically quench it\cite{xiIsingPairingSuperconducting2016}. In conventional superconductors, the Pauli paramagnetic limit\cite{makiPauliParamagnetismSuperconducting1964} defines the field at which the Zeeman energy overcomes the superconducting gap, but monolayer \ce{NbSe2} exhibits an in-plane upper critical field more than six times the Pauli limit due to Ising pairing. Understanding this further is important for the development of superconductors resilient to high magnetic fields.

Conventional superconductivity appears when the system respects both spatial inversion symmetry and time-reversal symmetry, and can then be described by BCS theory. The breaking of inversion symmetry leads to Ising like superconductivity already seen in \ce{NbSe2}. Generally, the breaking of time reversal symmetry by the Zeeman effect leads to an \gls{FFLO} state\cite{larkinNONUNIFORMSTATESUPERCONDUCTORS1964,fuldeSuperconductivityStrongSpinExchange1964}, characterized by a non-zero momentum for the Cooper pairs. The simultaneous breaking of these two symmetries can lead many possible finite-momentum pairing states\cite{liuUnconventionalSuperconductivityBilayer2017,wanOrbitalFuldeFerrell2023}. \ce{NbSe2} has been identified as a host for an orbital \gls{FFLO} state. Unlike conventional \gls{FFLO} states driven by the Zeeman effect, this state arises from the orbital effect of a parallel magnetic field. This orbital \gls{FFLO} state is characterized by finite-momentum Cooper pairings from adjacent layer with effects such as the breaking of both translational and rotational symmetries visible in the vortex dynamics and in-plane anisotropy and real-space phase oscillations of the order parameter\cite{wanOrbitalFuldeFerrell2023}. Detecting these effects is an experimental challenge that \gls{SQM} may be uniquely positioned to address.

\ce{NbSe2} is fundamentally a multi-gap superconductor, possessing a complex Fermi surface. Research suggests that superconductivity develops preferentially in the band associated with the large gap, while a smaller gap is induced in other bands through proximity effects in reciprocal space\cite{noatQuasiparticleSpectra22015,yokoyaFermiSurfaceSheetDependent2001}. The material also undergoes a charge density wave transition at \qty{30}{\kelvin}, characterized by a lattice distortion into a superstructure\cite{wilsonChargeDensityWavesMetallic1974,malliakasNbNbInteractions2013}. The interplay between the CDW and superconductivity remains an interesting question. Utilizing \gls{SQM} allows for the non-invasive probing of these phases.

The mapping of the magnetic field landscape of thin superconductors reveals important information the theoretical description of superconductivity\cite{clemSimpleModelVortex1975,lembergerPenetrationDepthStudy2007,gubinDependenceMagneticPenetration2005}. For few atomic layer superconductors, there can be a complicated magnetic landscape due to the geometry and the corresponding spatial variation of the order parameter. The study of the superconducting vortices and their interaction is a probe of this order parameter. \gls{SQM} offers a unique method to further explore the magnetic dynamics in superconductors, especially as function of temperature and magnetic field\cite{mongeSpinDynamicsSolidState2023,kolkowitzProbingJohnsonNoise2015,dolgirevCharacterizingTwodimensionalSuperconductivity2022b}. The long range interaction enabled in thin superconductors can also give rise to more complicated vortex-antivortex interactions\cite{beasleyPossibilityVortexAntivortexPair1979,curtisProbingBerezinskiiKosterlitzThoulessVortex2024}. \gls{SQM} can detect the superconductor transition by sensing the change in transverse noise due in the material due to electric current fluctuations, and its additional contribution to the probe's relaxation rate. By studying the noise as a function of temperature, NV centers can distinguish between nodal and non-nodal superconductors based on noise scaling\cite{chatterjeeSinglespinQubitMagnetic2022}.

In summary, the combination of \ce{NbSe2}'s unique properties, its complex multigap structure intertwined with CDW order, and its ability to host finite-momentum pairing—makes it an interesting candidate for exploring 2D superconductivity. \gls{SQM} offers a powerful, high-resolution method to unravel these complexities at the nanoscale

\section{Scanning quantum microscopy}

2H-\ce{NbSe2} is chosen as a prototypical layered 2D superconductor for investigation by \gls{SQM}. Bulk \ce{NbSe2} is a type II superconductor with a zero-field critical temperature of $\sim\qty{7}{\kelvin}$\cite{xiIsingPairingSuperconducting2016,tanakaMulticomponentSuperconductivityBased2015,zehetmayerExperimentalEvidenceTwoband2010}. It offers an accessible method of creating a few atomic layers devices via exfoliation like other 2D TMDs\cite{dvirSpectroscopyBulkFewlayer2018,songSynthesisAqueousAirstable2023}. With an interesting phase space occupied by melting transitions\cite{guillamonDirectObservationMelting2009}, charge density waves\cite{chikinaTurningChargedensityWaves2020}, and tilted vortex cores\cite{galvisTiltedVortexCores2018}, \ce{NbSe2} is an interesting choice for the study in this work. What follows here is the experimental study of various thin \ce{NbSe2} samples to glean its static and dynamic properties and how it is influenced by geometry, temperature and magnetic field.

\subsection{Mapping Meissner effect and vortices}

Local magnetic field measurements are performed on thin-layer samples of the two-dimensional superconductor 2H-\ce{NbSe2} utilizing the developed cryogenic \gls{AFM} and magnetometer. The measurements are performed at various temperatures, with the system achieving an operating base temperature of \qty{1.8}{\kelvin}, well below the superconducting transition temperature of $\sim\qty{6.5}{\kelvin}$\cite{zhangNonreciprocalSuperconductingNbSe22020}. 

The phase diagram for the thinner \ce{NbSe2} region is illustrated in \cref{fig:phase_diag}. The phase diagram shows three distinct regimes: the \gls{VG}, \gls{VL}, and metallic states. These phases are identified via transport measurements characterizing the transitions between superconducting and dissipative states\cite{zhangNonreciprocalSuperconductingNbSe22020}. In this context, $T_M$ denotes the melting temperature, while $H_{c1}$ and $H_{c2}$ correspond to the lower and upper critical fields of a type-II superconductor, respectively. It is noted that few layer \ce{NbSe2} shows a very low $H_{c1}$ of $\sim\unit{\micro\tesla}$, such that even the remnant field in the system is sufficient to introduce vortices.

The optical image and \gls{AFM} micrograph of the thin sample investigated is as shown in \cref{fig:s2_ovw}. As is often with 2D materials, the different thickness of the material are visible as optical contrast with \gls{DIC} microscopy. The fives sided flake is the sample with two visible thicker regions on the right and left. The entire sample is encapsulated with \ce{h-BN}, the edge of which is not visible in the optical image. The optical image shows a seemingly pristine sample. The \gls{AFM} scan tells a different story and shows the presence of bubbles and residue that is inherent to the 2D material transfer process used, rendering a challenging landscape for most scanning probes. 

The two distinct \ce{NbSe2} thickness regions in the flake are of the thickness $\qty{5.03}{\nano\meter}$ (corresponding to seven atomic layers) and $\qty{12.55}{\nano\meter}$. The thinner region is first addressed to see the existence of vortices at the 2D limit. From the \gls{AFM} scan it is seen that the top left of the flake in the thin region presents a good region for an \gls{SQM} scan due to the lack of large bubbles, and the possibility to scan over the edge of the sample. The \gls{SQM} scan allows the detection of local magnetic responses from the \ce{NbSe2} sample, through the protective hexagonal boron nitride \ce{h-BN} cladding.

Superimposed on this Meissner effect background, positive magnetic signals are seen. These are attributed to superconducting vortices, which persist across the majority of the superconducting phase space. Notably, vortex nucleation occurs at external field strengths as low as a few \unit{\milli\tesla}, a value significantly below the first critical field ($H_{c1}$) reported for bulk \ce{NbSe2}. \subsection{Single vortex mapping}

The vertical confinement of the thin \ce{NbSe2} layers introduces an increase in the size of the vortices, consistent with the Pearl vortex model\cite{pearlCURRENTDISTRIBUTIONSUPERCONDUCTING1964}. High-resolution scanning of a single vortex (\cref{fig:s2_single}) confirms this profile; the vortex exhibits a spatial extent exceeding that of bulk vortices, aligning with the scaling behaviour predicted for Pearl vortices in thin-film superconductors\cite{fridmanAnomalousThicknessDependence2025}, well above the spatial resolution of the \gls{SQM} probe $(\sim\qty{30}{\nano\meter})$. 

Post-fabrication characterization via standard \gls{AFM} (\cref{fig:s2_ovw}) indicates the presence of surface residue on the flake. While such surface contamination presents significant technical impediments for tunnelling-based probes like \gls{STM}\cite{zomerFastPickTechnique2014}, the \gls{SQM} technique employed here robustness to such samples. \gls{SQM} enables high-resolution, non-invasive mapping of superconducting vortices despite surface imperfections. Although residues may induce minor artifacts in the magnetic field map, these deviations are systematically correctable a-posteriori by correlating the magnetic data with \gls{AFM} topography or can be simply accounted for during data interpretation. Some situations can arise where residue can attach itself onto the tip of the scanning probe and render it unable to perform measurements. Although seldom, this does happen with the \gls{NV} \gls{SQM} but can simply be removed by performing a fast scan with a harder than usual \gls{AFM} contact force. The picked up residue shows itself as a sudden increase in topography and loss of \gls{SQM} resolution.

\subsection{Thickness dependence}

In the regime of few-layer \ce{NbSe2}, the superconducting response is strongly influenced by substrate roughness, structural disorder, and finite-size effects. These local perturbations induce significant deviations from the canonical vortex profile and arrangements typically observed in pristine superconductors, resulting in distorted and anisotropic vortex shapes. Furthermore, the multi-band nature of superconductivity in \ce{NbSe2}\cite{noatQuasiparticleSpectra22015,zehetmayerExperimentalEvidenceTwoband2010} allows for the potential stabilization of multi-flux-quanta vortices\cite{gozlinskiBandresolvedCaroliGennes2023}.

\subsection{Vortex arrangement over the sample}

To further explore the influence of the sample boundaries, thickness without change in encapsulation, and distribution of disorder in the sample, a large \gls{SQM} scan is performed on sample S2. The sample is cooled following a slow field cooling procedure from above $T_C$ to \qty{3.1}{\kelvin} with an applied field of \qty{165}{\milli\tesla} along the \gls{NV} axis. \subsection{Vortex melting phase}

Transport studies on thin \ce{NbSe2} samples have provided indirect evidence regarding the vortex glass melting transition. These studies suggest that melting occurs more easily in thin layers than in bulk systems. In two-dimensional superconductors, thermal fluctuations have a greater effect; these fluctuations can de-pin vortices from their fixed positions and drive the transition into a liquid state\cite{zhangNonreciprocalSuperconductingNbSe22020}. Experimentally, this is observed as a non-zero resistivity appearing across a broad temperature window well below the critical temperature. This resistive signal indicates that vortices have detached from their pinning sites and entered a mobile, vortex liquid phase, contributing to electrical resistance. Furthermore, structural defects such as dislocations play a critical role in this process\cite{guillamonDirectObservationMelting2009}. Due to this very mobility in this phase, the final state of the vortex glass is highly dependent on the speed at which the sample is cooled through the vortex liquid phase\cite{jayaramProbingVortexDynamics2025}.

To investigate this dependence, a systematic study using two cooling rates is performed. For each sequence, the sample temperature is first increased to \qty{6.3}{\kelvin}. While this temperature is below the superconducting transition, the magnetic field response is very weak, and large-area scans confirm that isolated vortices are no longer visible. This acts as a reset for the vortex configuration by entering into the end of the liquid phase, allowing for de-pinning of the vortices.

From this reset state, the sample is cooled down to the base temperature of \qty{2}{\kelvin}. This procedure is repeated for two cooling rates, namely, a rapid \qty{1}{\minute} cooling and a slow \qty{10}{\hour} cooling.

The \gls{SQM} magnetometry results for the two cooling rates are presented in \cref{fig:fast_slow}. Although these measurements focus on the exact same location of the few-layer sample, the resulting magnetic maps differ significantly. We observe distinct differences in the vortex arrangement and intensity. The difference in the final vortex state can be explained by the competition between thermal fluctuations and disorder pinning.

In the case of a rapid cooling from the melted state, the vortices do not have time to settle into an equilibrium configuration. The abrupt drop in temperature causes them to be immediately pinned by random disorder sites present in the material. These randomly pinned vortices remain volatile and are more susceptible to local thermal fluctuations. This results in a configuration where vortices are delocalized on a small length scale, leading to the observed weaker overall magnetic response.

In contrast, the slow cooling acts as an annealing process. It allows the system to remain in thermal equilibrium for longer. This gives thermal fluctuations enough time to overcome local disorder pinning, allowing the vortices to gradually arrange themselves into a more stable, energetically favourable lattice configuration\cite{jayaramProbingVortexDynamics2025}.

To further understand this behaviour, a similar measurement is performed near the step-edge on the sample. This should also give insight into the vortex behaviour at the edge and its vicinity, as well as a comparison between two thicknesses. \cref{fig:fast_slow_large} shows the \gls{SQM} magnetometry results from this region. The step edge is further investigated in a later section (\cref{sec:step}), so the discussion is limited here to the melting behaviour. A rearrangement of the vortex positions is also seen. In contrast, the slow cooling scan shows vortices at the same area showing a more regular vortex shape. The length of this line is affected by the cooling rate and shows a shorter length for the slow cooldown. This phenomenon is currently not well understood and is further discussed in a later section.

\subsection{Vortex state - dynamics}

Beyond DC magnetometry, the \gls{NV} center enables dynamic sensing across a broad frequency range\cite{barrySensitivityOptimizationNVdiamond2020}. By employing tailored spin control pulse sequences, specific noise sources affecting the spin can be spectrally isolated or decoupled. This capability renders \gls{NV}-based techniques essential for investigating fluctuations in 2D superconductors, ranging from DC to gigahertz frequencies. Several works using single \gls{NV} centers are sensitive probes of dynamics behaviour are a reported with even more being proposed\cite{kolkowitzProbingJohnsonNoise2015,curtisProbingBerezinskiiKosterlitzThoulessVortex2024,dolgirevCharacterizingTwodimensionalSuperconductivity2022b}. The expectation of noise from normal conductors is quite well understood. Johnson noise stems from thermally induced electrical currents that cause fluctuating electric and magnetic field in proximity to a conductor. This noise in the \unit{\giga\hertz} range is sensed using the $T_1$ relaxation of the \gls{NV} center. Due to the vanishing resistance in superconductors, the noise spectrum is different. \gls{NV} centers are reported probing the noise environment in superconductors\cite{mongeSpinDynamicsSolidState2023}, but the works are limited to thick superconductors or low spatial resolution. Nevertheless, the results mostly show the increase of the shallow \gls{NV} centre coherence times which is attributed the presence of mirror charges at the surface of the superconductor. These results present a different conclusion than what is seen here.

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/noise_single.pdf}

(b) Hahn echo $T_2$ measurements with the \gls{NV} probe in \gls{AFM} contact with the sample and retracted $\sim\qty{2}{\micro\meter}$ from the sample. In superconductors, significant fluctuations typically arise from the thermal excitation of quasiparticles\cite{chatterjeeSinglespinQubitMagnetic2022,dolgirevCharacterizingTwodimensionalSuperconductivity2022b} or variations in the superconducting order parameter\cite{curtisProbingBerezinskiiKosterlitzThoulessVortex2024}. Fluctuations in the supercurrent generate transverse magnetic noise, leading to the dephasing of the \gls{NV} electron spin when it is in proximity. This dephasing can be characterized using Hahn-echo $T_2$ measurements.

An advantage of \gls{SQM} is the convenient control of sensor-sample distance. To confirm the presence of magnetic noise from the superconductor, the spin coherence measurement is performed with probe in contact with the sample, and retracted ($\sim\qty{2}{\micro\meter}$). \cref{fig:noiseB} shows the $T_2$ curves under both conditions. A distinct reduction in $T_2$ is observed when in contact with the sample, and a reproducible revival of coherence when retracted. To further understand the spatial extent of the magnetic noise, lift-off measurement is performed. The Hahn-echo $T_2$ curves are collected with increasing sensor-sample separation, starting from in-contact. The extracted coherence times is shown in \cref{fig:noiseLift}. The effect due to the sample vanishes at separations greater than $\sim\qty{100}{\nano\meter}$, suggesting also a characteristic length scale for the noise source, such as the motion of vortices. Due to the spectral filtering of the pulse sequence, the noise is understood to be within the kilohertz to megahertz frequency band.

(b) The enhanced decoherence rate between in-contact and out-of contact conditions, measured below the superconducting transition temperature. A further counterintuitive result is obtained studying the temperature dependence of the magnetic noise. The initial expectations are the superconducting state to be a low-magnetic noise phase when compared to a conductor\cite{ariyaratneNanoscaleElectricalConductivity2018}. \cref{fig:noiseTemp} shows the decoherence caused by proximity to the superconductor as a function of temperature. The data show that the noise-induced decoherence is largest at low temperatures and diminishes as the temperature approaches $T_c$. It is expected that order parameter fluctuations peak near $T_c$\cite{curtisProbingBerezinskiiKosterlitzThoulessVortex2024}, and quasiparticle excitations should reduce with decreasing temperature\cite{dolgirevCharacterizingTwodimensionalSuperconductivity2022b}. However, the Hahn-echo $T_2$ sequence is primarily sensitive to frequencies in the megahertz range, unlike the typically investigated $T_1$. Then, it is likely that the dominant factor limiting $T_2$ is the thermally activated motion of the vortex glass. The vortex motion itself reduce as the temperature lowers, showing also the transition from vortex liquid to glass state.

Instead, this noise is driven by the temperature dependence of the penetration depth, $\lambda$. Although vortex motion slows down at lower temperatures, $\lambda$ decreases, causing the magnetic field distribution around individual vortices to become more localized. Consequently, the local magnetic field at the \gls{NV} center becomes increasingly sensitive to even small changes in vortex position. This is validated using a Langevin equation, as a simple model for vortex motion at finite temperature.

\subsection*{Theoretical model\footnotemark}
\footnotetext{The theoretical work is mainly done by Lucas Vieira Pupim (Insitute for Theoretical Physics III, Universität Stuttgart)}
The idea of the model is to introduce a single vortex as a source of magnetic noise to the \gls{NV} center a finite stand-off distance away. Although multiple vortices are present in the vicinity of the \gls{NV} center, only a single vortex is described here and along with spatial resolution considerations, this is sufficient to understand the underlying mechanism. This gives the tools to understand whether the experimentally observed noise signature is commensurate with the current interpretation. To set the stage, first the \gls{NV} center is described. The position of the \gls{NV} center is at $\mathbf{r}_0 = (0,0,z_0)^{\mathrm{T}}$. The spin state splitting is $\Omega$, quantization axis is $\mathbf{n}_q$, with coupling to a magnetic field $\mathbf{B(r)}$. The \gls{NV} center Hamiltonian is then given by
\begin{equation}
    H = \frac{\Omega}{2} \boldsymbol{\sigma} \cdot \mathbf{n}_q + \frac{\gamma}{2} \mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}_0, t) \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma}.
\end{equation}

Now, the magnetic field distribution around a single vortex is described as by \citeauthor*{kosslerMagneticFieldsVortices2012}. A cylindrical coordinate system is used with coordinates $\rho, \theta, z$. The vortex is located at $\rho = 0$ in a thin superconductor of thickness $d$, between two infinite air regions. The vector potential satisfies the following equation 

\begin{equation}
    \nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf{A}) + \frac{\mathbf{A}}{\lambda^2} = \frac{\phi_0 \hat{\theta}}{2\pi \lambda^2 r},
\end{equation}
for $\phi_0 = \qty{2.07e3}{G\kilo\angstrom\squared}$.

For superconducting penetration depth $\lambda$, Bessel function $J_1(\gamma\rho)$, and $s = (\gamma^2 + 1/\gamma^2)^{1/2}$, the magnetic field can then be obtained from the vector potential $\mathbf{A} = f\hat{\theta}$ with
\begin{equation}
    f(\rho, z) = \frac{\phi_0}{2\pi\lambda^2} \int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{J_1(\gamma\rho)}{s^2} \frac{ (1 - e^{-sd}) e^{\gamma(z + d/2)}s}{s(1 - e^{-sd}) + \gamma(1 + e^{-sd})} d\gamma ,
\end{equation}

which is a solution for this functional form of the vector potential when outside the superconductor at $z>d/2$. All length scales will be measured in terms of $\lambda_0 = \qty{325}{\nano\meter}$, giving thickness $d = 0.025\lambda_0$, tip stand-off $z_0 = 0.16\lambda_0$. This value of $\lambda_0$ best fits the measurements and is in good agreement with \citeauthor*{fridmanAnomalousThicknessDependence2025}. To introduce temperature dependence, the temperature dependant penetration depth $\lambda(T) = \frac{\lambda_0}{\sqrt{1-(T/T_C)^4}}$\cite{prozorovMagneticPenetrationDepth2006}. The time dependence of the magnetic field, due to the vortex motion is introduced now. For a vortex confined to the $z=0$ plane, we have $\mathbf{x}_v(t) \in \mathbb{R}^2$. To include the neighbouring vortices and their effect, a harmonic potential is considered. The restoring force is given by $\mathbf{F} = -k(\mathbf{x}_v - \mathbf{x}_{v,0})$. The thermal fluctuations of the vortices are introduces as a time-dependent, uncorrelated random force $\boldsymbol{\eta}(t)$. These fluctuations perturb the vortex and the dynamics is described by the corrector $\langle \boldsymbol{\eta}(t) \boldsymbol{\eta}(t') \rangle = 2D \delta(t - t')$, with diffusion constant $D$. Combining all these contributions in the form of the Langevin equation\cite{holmlundLangevinSimulationsTwodimensional1996}, we have

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \frac{d\mathbf{x}_v(t)}{dt} &= D \mathbf{F}_{\text{tot}}(t) + \boldsymbol{\eta}(t) \\
         &= -\frac{Dk}{T} \left( \mathbf{x}_v(t) - \mathbf{x}_{v,0} \right) + \boldsymbol{\eta}(t).
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
This equation described the strong friction limit for in plane motion of vortices and is a good approximation of a 2D superconductor\cite{holmlundLangevinSimulationsTwodimensional1996}. This then solved using the Euler-Maruyama method\cite{bayerDiscretizationSDEsEuler}. From fitting to measurements the constants are found to be $D\cdot k = \qty{0.11}{\kelvin\micro\second}^{-1}$, and $|\mathbf{x}_{v,0}| \approx \qty{260}{\nano\meter}$. This equation gives the trajectory of a vortex in the superconductor and consequently the trajectory of the magnetic field source. Since the trajectory takes $\boldsymbol{\eta}(t)$ as a variable, $\mathbf{B}(\mathbf{r}_0, t)$ becomes a stochastic variable.

With the vortex dynamics and resulting magnetic field established, the effect on the \gls{NV} center can be calculated. This is done by calculating the coherence function under a spin-echo protocol. This follows the description by \citeauthor*{cywinskiHowEnhanceDephasing2008}, and the same conventions are used here.

The interaction between a superconducting qubit and its environment is modelled using a Hamiltonian where the energy relaxation time ($T_1$) is assumed to be much longer than the dephasing time ($T_2$). This is expressed as
\begin{equation}
    H = \frac{1}{2} (\Omega + \beta(t)) \sigma_z,
\end{equation}
with $\Omega$ being the spin state splitting , $\beta(t)$ a random variable representing the temporal fluctuations in that splitting caused by environmental noise, and $\sigma_z$ a Pauli matrix. $\beta(t)$ represents a stochastic process.

To measure dephasing, the qubit is initially prepared in a coherent superposition of $\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow}$ states:
\begin{equation}
    |\psi(0)\rangle = a\ket{\uparrow} + b\ket{\downarrow}.
\end{equation}

This state is typically created by applying a $\pi/2$ rotation to an eigenstate of $\sigma_z$, placing the Bloch vector in the $xy$ plane. In an \gls{FID} experiment, the off-diagonal element of the qubit's density matrix ($\rho_{+-}$) evolves as:
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{+-}^{\text{FID}}(t) = e^{-i\Omega t} e^{\left(-i\int_{0}^{t} \beta(t') \, dt'\right)} \rho_{+-}(0).
\end{equation}

The second exponential term represents the random phase accumulation caused by the noise $\beta(t)$. 

The coherence function $W(t)$ is then defined by taking the average of the off-diagonal elements:

\begin{equation}
    W(t) = \frac{\lvert \langle \rho_{+-}(t) \rangle \rvert}{\lvert \langle \rho_{+-}(0) \rangle \rvert}.
\end{equation}

 When ideal $\pi$ pulses are applied at specific times $(t_1, \dots, t_n)$, they flip the amplitudes of the $\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow}$ states. This modifies the coherence function into:
\begin{equation}
    W(t) = \left\lvert\left\langle \exp\left[-i \int_{0}^{t} \xi(t') f(t; t') \, dt' \right] \right\rangle\right\rvert.
\end{equation}

The function $f(t; t')$ is the time-domain filter function that alternates between $+1$ and $-1$ at each $\pi$ pulse, effectively refocusing the phase accumulation. 

Using this function with the previously described fluctuating vortex gives us 
\begin{equation}
    W(t) = \left\lvert\left\langle \exp\left[-i \int_{0}^{t} B_z(\mathbf{r}_0,t') f(t; t') \, dt' \right] \right\rangle\right\rvert.
\end{equation}

With the coherence function at hand, the characteristic decay time $T_2$ defined as the time when $W(T_2) = 1/e$, is calculated. The coherence considerations so far only account for the component of the magnetic field along the \gls{NV} axis, which is along $z$. In plane components of the magnetic field is considered to be averaging to zero due to the many neighbouring vortices. The simulation is performed for time $t$ up to $\qty{20}{\micro\second}$. When approaching $T_c$, the $T_2$ time increases beyond the simulation time and therefore is calculated from a fit. The expression is averaged over \qty{60000}{samples}.

Although the individual considerations are simple, they provide a sufficient description of the experiment and successfully reproduces the inverse temperature dependence observed in the experimental data, confirming the origin of the noise.

\section{Step junctions in a 2D superconductor}\label{sec:step}

The study of 2D superconductors frequently encounters structural inhomogeneities inherent to the mechanical exfoliation process. This is detailed in the previous section. These inhomogeneities are largely ignored for investigator using non-local and spatially averaging probes, but it can be seen that the local arrangement of vortices and the superconducting order parameter is strong influenced by the inhomogeneities and finite spatial extent of the flake\cite{jayaramProbingVortexDynamics2025}.  Unlike epitaxial films, exfoliated flakes often present a terraced morphology characterized by step junctions. These junctions are interfaces where the layer count, and thus the physical thickness, changes abruptly over a sub-nanometer vertical scale\cite{fridmanAnomalousThicknessDependence2025}. These junctions pose a challenge for non-local probes as the measured properties have their sensors over two sample thickness and their transition regions. Nevertheless, with spatial resolution these junctions provide a unique experimental platform. They allow for the simultaneous interrogation of two distinct superconducting regions within a single exfoliated crystal, facilitating a direct comparison of their magnetic and electronic responses under identical conditions. The transition region between the two regions can also present unique phenomena. 

In 2D superconductors where the film thickness $d$ is significantly smaller than the bulk London penetration depth $\lambda$, magnetic field screening becomes markedly less efficient. In this regime, the characteristic length scale for magnetic field distribution is governed by the Pearl length $\Lambda = \frac{2\lambda^2}{d}$. The Pearl length is inversely proportional to the film thickness, meaning that variations in the number of atomic layers result in changes to the vortex magnetic profile. Consequently, a step junction represents a spatial discontinuity in the superfluid density. \gls{SQM} imaging reveals that vortices in thinner regions exhibit broader, more diffuse magnetic field distributions compared to those in thicker terraces, where screening is more robust\cite{jayaramProbingVortexDynamics2025,fridmanAnomalousThicknessDependence2025}. 

Step junctions act as geometric barriers or pinning centers for magnetic vortices. As a vortex approaches a step, it encounters a gradient in its self-energy due to the change in $\Lambda$ and the associated magnetic flux distribution\cite{agassiThicknessdependentPinningSuperconductor2007}. This interaction can lead to the accumulation of vortices along the step edge, where the energetic cost of crossing the thickness discontinuity is maximized. Furthermore, the asymmetry at these junctions can induce non-reciprocal vortex motion, a phenomenon recently identified as the superconducting vortex diode effect\cite{gutfreundDirectObservationSuperconducting2023}. This asymmetry could alter the reciprocity of the critical current required to depin a vortex and become dependent on the direction of the Lorentz force relative to the step geometry\cite{suriNonreciprocityVortexlimitedCritical2022,leeReducingVortexDensity1999}.

At the interface of a step junction, the superconducting wavefunction from the adjacent regions must satisfy boundary conditions that enforce phase coherence. If the transition between thicknesses is sufficiently sharp, the junction may behave as a weak link, hosting Josephson-like dynamics\cite{yabukiSupercurrentVanWaals2016}. Magnetic imaging provides insights into the proximity effects between layers of varying thickness at and away from the junctions. \gls{SQM} provides an excellent tool to map the static and dynamic magnetic signatures with high spatial resolution and over a wide range of temperatures and magnetic fields. The probe is not affected by large step sizes or residue and bubbles that are a consequence of exfoliation processes.

The step junction is initially identified using a confocal scan of the sample. The optical microscope image of the sample can identify under \gls{DIC} contrast the difference in layer count. Identifying markers such as bubbles and residue is used to compare with the confocal image, since these markers often show as bright features. Once the step edge is located the, the \gls{SQM} scan is performed. An \gls{SQM} scan on relatively thick \ce{NbSe2} flake is shown in \cref{fig:step_s6}. A slow field cooling procedure from above $T_c$ is performed to ensure a relaxed vortex lattice and reproducible measurement conditions. The temperature is also set to be above the base temperature of the measurement cryostat to ensure sufficient cooling power to the sample and probe, and therefore a reliable and accurate temperature throughout the measurement.

\subsection*{Magnetic field dependence - step junction in thin \ce{NbSe2}}

The difference in the magnetic field response between thin and thick \ce{NbSe2} can be strong (see \cref{fig:thick_thin}). The vortices appear in a more irregular pattern and the Meissner response is weaker. The magnetic signature of such a flake along with a step edge is interesting. \cref{fig:step_fields} show the \gls{SQM} magnetometry of a flake of thickness $\qty{12.55}{\nano\meter}$ corresponding to 17 layers in the thick region and $\qty{5.03}{\nano\meter}$ corresponding to 5 layers in the thin region, the same sample as investigated in \cref{fig:vortices_s2}. 

The measurements here are performed under slow field cooling to \qty{3.1}{\kelvin} at various bias magnetic fields. The number of vortices increase with increasing applied magnetic field, as is expected. The other observation is the increased measurement noise in the magnetometry. Here, and spatial smoothening is avoided deliberately, and the raw data presented for two reasons. One, the step junction shows a significant merging of vortices and this effect can be misrepresented if smoothened using a filter to reduce noise. Second, it is important to note that \ce{NbSe2}, especially at the few layer limit, exhibit quenching of the \gls{NV} center contrast when in contact, therefore increasing the measurement noise due to a reduced \gls{SNR}. This effect is not well understood and requires further investigation. Some preliminary measurements attempting to understand this is presented in \cref{app:contrast}. 

For all applied magnetic fields in \cref{fig:step_fields}, the Meissner repulsion is stronger on the thicker side, with more distinctly separated vortices. The accumulation of vortices at the step edge is also present. Even at lower fields with low vortex density, as in \cref{fig:step_fields_a}, with an applied magnetic field of \qty{24}{\milli\tesla}, the vortices are seemingly elongated and merging. The effect is enhanced or simply more visible at higher vortex densities. 

One possible explanation is supported by the previously described motion vortices, and the time averaged nature of the \gls{SQM} scan. Since the vortices are understood to be moving, the step edge could act as a site where the motion is enhanced along the direction of the step edge. This effect as seen by an averaging sensor, could appear as an elongated vortex. 

At the maximum applied field of \qty{200}{\milli\tesla} in \cref{fig:step_fields_d}, the number of irregular appearing vortices is significant. At the step edge, individual and vortex shaped features are not visible anymore.

Besides the shape of these vortices, it is noted that the magnetic field measured in the vortex region is not uniform. This directly relates to the enclosed magnetic flux quantum. The fundamental property of a superconductor is the quantization of vorticity\cite{onsagerStatisticalHydrodynamics1949,londonProblemMolecularTheory1948,abrikosovMagneticPropertiesSuperconductors1957}, or the magnetic flux of these vortices. The magnetic flux $\Phi$ is defined as the integral of the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ over the sample, i.e., $\Phi = \int dxdy \mathbf{B} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}$, for the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ normal to the sample surface. The implication is the quantization of magnetic field penetrating a superconductor, i.e., the magnetic field in a type-II superconductor increases in steps of the magnetic flux quanta $\Phi_0 = hc/2e$ (CGS units)\cite{abrikosovMagneticPropertiesSuperconductors1957}. These vortices also show a phase change of $2\pi$ of the complex superconducting order parameter around vortex core. This quantization holds true for the variety of superconducting materials with integer flux quanta, irrespective of other superconducting properties, material, composition, temperature, purity, etc.\cite{iguchiSuperconductingVorticesCarrying2023}. This is supported by many experimental works\cite{changScanningHallProbe1992,hessScanningtunnelingmicroscopyStudyDistortion1992,schlusselWideFieldImagingSuperconductor2018}. Despite the established universality of the integer flux quantization, there are reports where it is possible to have vortex excitations that carry a fraction of the flux quantum. This fraction is not a rational number, and exhibits continuous change dependent on temperature, challenging the universality of the flux quantization\cite{iguchiSuperconductingVorticesCarrying2023}.

\section*{Conclusion and outlook}

Nanoscale spatiotemporal imaging of vortices in few-layer 2D superconductors is developed, enabling direct visualization of vortex phenomena previously accessible only through indirect measurement signatures. Geometry and 2D superconductor specific vortex arrangement, deformed vortices, direct evidence of vortex melting and the vortex glass and liquid phase, where the configuration is highly cooling-rate dependent are imaged. This reflects the interplay of thermal fluctuations and pinning sites. 

The dynamics of the superconductor spatially and over temperature is investigated, revealing unexpected noise. Both the spatial arrangement and dynamic behaviour of vortices at low magnetic fields have significant implications for the collective macroscopic response of the system, as measured by transport, nuclear magnetic resonance, and other indirect techniques. This work establishes direct imaging of superconductors, particularly with \gls{SQM} to be an essential tool for the study of solid state physics.

Step junctions in \ce{NbSe2} are investigated where unusual vortex arrangements and non-uniform magnetic flux signatures are measured. The arrangement of vortices along the step edges are noted. The results observed here entertain a conclusion of non-quantized magnetic flux, calling for a thorough investigation of more samples at various field and temperature conditions. An additional step junction in the vicinity immediately presents more complex behaviour (see \cref{fig:large_step_app}). The dynamics studied so far have been confined to single spatial point measurements. Further, twisted superconducting materials also prove an interesting direction, considering the layered nature of superconductivity seen in \ce{NbSe2}.

Initial experiments with spatial coherence scan protocol show that the spatial variation of broadband noise from thin samples is non-trivial. Mapping of this spatial noise with probes having long coherence times and isotopic purification present an interesting direction. The layered nature of this superconductor further allows for the study of complex junctions at various step sizes and angle, twisting between layers, and heterostructures.
