\subsection{Mapping Meissner effect and vortices}

Local magnetic field measurements are performed on thin-layer samples of the two-dimensional superconductor 2H-\ce{NbSe2} utilizing the developed cryogenic atomic force microscope (AFM) and magnetometer, operating at a base temperature of 1.8 K. The sample investigated is as depicted in \cref{fig:s2_ovw}. The different large flake of the material also has two thicker regions towards the edge, visible as different contrast in the micrograph. The AFM scan shows the presence of bubbles and residue that is inherent to the vdW material transfer process used, rendering a challenging landscape for most scanning probes.

A single Nitrogen-Vacancy (NV) center, facilitates the detection of local magnetic responses from the \ce{NbSe2} sample, through the protective hexagonal boron nitride \ce{h-BN} cladding. The \ce{NbSe2} two distinct thickness regions in th flake are: $\qty{5.03}{\nano\meter}$ (corresponding to seven atomic layers) and $\qty{12.55}{\nano\meter}$.

The phase diagram for the thinner \ce{NbSe2} region, illustrated in \cref{fig:phase_diag}, delineates three distinct regimes: the vortex glass (VG), vortex liquid (VL), and metallic (M) states. These phases were identified via transport measurements characterizing the transitions between superconducting and dissipative states\cite{zhangNonreciprocalSuperconductingNbSe22020}. In this context, $T_M$ denotes the melting temperature, while $H_{c1}$ and $H_{c2}$ correspond to the lower and upper critical fields of a type-II superconductor, respectively.

\cref{fig:s2_ovw} displays a magnetic scan acquired at the metallic phase and at \qty{2}{\kelvin}, within the vortex glass state. The data reveals a pronounced diamagnetic response along the flake edge, indicative of the Meissner effect. Control measurements taken at \qty{10}{\kelvin} ($T > T_c$) show a complete vanishing of this magnetic response, confirming that the signals observed at \qty{2}{\kelvin} originate exclusively from the superconducting state.

Due to the geometric confinement of the sample, where the flake thickness is significantly less than the London penetration depth of bulk \ce{NbSe2} ($\lambda \approx \qty{125}{\nano\meter}$)\cite{callaghanFieldDependenceVortex2005,fletcherPenetrationDepthStudy2007}, the external magnetic field is only partially screened. The effective field screening is approximately \qty{-100}{\micro\tesla}. 

Superimposed on this background, positive magnetic signals are observed and attributed to superconducting vortices, which persist across the majority of the superconducting phase space. Notably, vortex nucleation occurs at external field strengths as low as a few \unit{\milli\tesla}, a value significantly below the first critical field ($H_{c1}$) reported for bulk \ce{NbSe2}.

This premature vortex formation is a consequence of the sample's few-layer thickness, which permits magnetic field penetration and local suppression of the superconducting order parameter.

The vertical confinement inherent to the thin \ce{NbSe2} layers induces a radial expansion of the vortices, consistent with the Pearl vortex model\cite{pearlCURRENTDISTRIBUTIONSUPERCONDUCTING1964}. High-resolution scanning of a single vortex (\cref{fig:s2_single}) confirms this profile; the vortex exhibits a spatial extent exceeding that of bulk vortices, aligning with the scaling behavior predicted for Pearl vortices in thin-film superconductors\cite{fridmanAnomalousThicknessDependence2025}. 

Post-fabrication characterization via AFM (\cref{fig:s2_ovw}) indicates the presence of surface residue on the flake. While such surface contamination presents significant technical impediments for tunneling-based probes like Scanning Tunneling Microscopy (STM)\cite{zomerFastPickTechnique2014}, the Scanning Quantum Microscopy (SQM) technique employed here demonstrates superior robustness. SQM enables high-resolution, non-invasive mapping of superconducting vortices despite surface imperfections. Although residues may induce minor artifacts in the magnetic field map, these deviations are systematically correctable a-posteriori by correlating the magnetic data with AFM topography.

In the regime of few-layer \ce{NbSe2}, the superconducting response is strongly influenced by substrate roughness, structural disorder, and finite-size effects. These local perturbations induce significant deviations from the canonical vortex profile and arrangements typically observed in pristine superconductors, resulting in distorted and anisotropic vortex cores. Furthermore, the multi-band nature of superconductivity in \ce{NbSe2}\cite{noatQuasiparticleSpectra22015,zehetmayerExperimentalEvidenceTwoband2010} allows for the potential stabilization of multi-flux-quanta vortices\cite{gozlinskiBandresolvedCaroliGennes2023}.

This lack of long-range order is revealed via autocorrelation analysis in \cref{fig:thinB}, which exhibits elongated and irregular correlation peaks. While a nascent hexagonal symmetry is discernible, the arrangement is disordered. The vortex lattice topology is also strongly influenced by the sample and step edge boundaries, with autocorrelation features aligning along the edges. This is frequently overlooked yet may be important for a proper deduction of the symmetry of the superconducting ground state from such experimental data. Consequently, characterizing the specific, non-hexagonal vortex arrangement is essential for the accurate deduction of the superconducting ground state symmetry\cite{zhangNonreciprocalSuperconductingNbSe22020,noatQuasiparticleSpectra22015}.

Transport studies on thin \ce{NbSe2} samples have provided indirect evidence regarding the vortex glass melting transition. These studies suggest that melting occurs more easily in thin layers than in bulk systems. In two-dimensional superconductors, thermal fluctuations have a greater effect; these fluctuations can de-pin vortices from their fixed positions and drive the transition into a liquid state\cite{zhangNonreciprocalSuperconductingNbSe22020}. Experimentally, this is observed as a non-zero resistivity appearing across a broad temperature window well below the critical temperature. This resistive signal indicates that vortices have detached from their pinning sites and entered a mobile, vortex liquid phase, contributing to electrical resistance. Furthermore, structural defects such as dislocations play a critical role in this process\cite{guillamonDirectObservationMelting2009}. Due to this very mobility in this phase, the final state of the vortex glass is highly dependent on the speed at which the sample is cooled through the vortex liquid phase\cite{jayaramProbingVortexDynamics2025}.

To investigate this dependence, a systematic study using two cooling rates is performed. For each sequence, we first raised the sample temperature to \qty{6.3}{\kelvin}. While this temperature is below the superconducting transition, the magnetic field response is very weak, and large-area scans confirm that isolated vortices are no longer visible. This acts as a reset for the vortex configuration by entering into the end of the liquid phase

From this reset state, the sample is cooled down to the base temperature of \qty{2}{\kelvin}. This procedure is repeated for two cooling rates, namely, a rapid \qty{1}{\minute} cooling to a slow \qty{10}{\hour} cooling.

The SQM results for the two cooling rates are presented in \cref{fig:fast_slow}. Although these measurements focus on the exact same location of the few-layer sample, the resulting magnetic maps differ significantly. We observe distinct differences in the vortex arrangement and intensity. The difference in the final vortex state can be explained by the competition between thermal fluctuations and disorder pinning.

In the case of a rapid cooling (quenching) from the melted state, the vortices do not have time to settle into an equilibrium configuration. The abrupt drop in temperature causes them to be immediately pinned by random disorder sites present in the material. These randomly pinned vortices remain volatile and are more susceptible to local thermal fluctuations. This results in a configuration where vortices are delocalized on a small length scale, leading to the observed weaker overall magnetic response.

In contrast, the slow cooling acts as an annealing process. It allows the system to remain in thermal equilibrium for longer. This gives thermal fluctuations enough time to overcome local disorder pinning, allowing the vortices to gradually arrange themselves into a more stable, energetically favorable lattice configuration\cite{jayaramProbingVortexDynamics2025}.

\subsection{Vortex state - dynamics}

Beyond DC magnetometry, the NV center enables dynamic sensing across a broad frequency range\cite{barrySensitivityOptimizationNVdiamond2020}. By employing tailored spin control pulse sequences, specific noise sources affecting the spin can be spectrally isolated or decoupled. This capability renders NV-based techniques essential for investigating fluctuations in 2D superconductors, ranging from DC to gigahertz frequencies.

In superconductors, significant fluctuations typically arise from the thermal excitation of quasiparticles\cite{chatterjeeSinglespinQubitMagnetic2022,dolgirevCharacterizingTwodimensionalSuperconductivity2022b} or variations in the superconducting order parameter\cite{curtisProbingBerezinskiiKosterlitzThoulessVortex2024}. Fluctuations in the supercurrent generate transverse magnetic noise, leading to the dephasing of the NV electron spin when it is in proximity. This dephasing can be characterized using Hahn-echo $T_2$ measurements.

An advantage of SQM is the convenient control of sensor-sample distance. To confirm the presence of magnetic noise from the superconductor, the spin coherence measurement is performed with probe in contact with the sample, and retracted ($\sim\qty{2}{\micro\meter}$). \cref{fig:noiseB} shows the $T_2$ curves under both conditions. A distinct reduction in $T_2$ is observed when in contact with the sample, and a reproducible revival of coherence when retracted. To further understand the spatial extent of the magnetic noise, lift-off measurement is performed. The Hahn-echo $T_2$ curves are collected with increasing sensor-sample separation, starting from in-contact. The extracted coherence times is shown in \cref{fig:noiseLift}. The effect due to the sample vanishes at separations greater than $\sim\qty{100}{\nano\meter}$, suggesting also a characteristic length scale for the noise source, such as the motion of vortices. Due to the spectral filtering of the pulse sequence, the noise is understood to be within the kilohertz to megahertz frequency band.

(b) The enhanced decoherence rate between in-contact and out-of contact conditions, measured below the superconducting transition temperature.     \refstepcounter{subfigure}\label{fig:noiseLift}
    \refstepcounter{subfigure}\label{fig:noiseTemp}

A further counterintuitive result is obtained studying the temperature dependence of the magnetic noise. The initial expectations are the superconducting state to be a low-magnetic noise phase when compared to a conductor\cite{ariyaratneNanoscaleElectricalConductivity2018}. \cref{fig:noiseTemp} shows the decoherence caused by proximity to the superconductor as a function of temperature. The data show that the noise-induced decoherence is largest at low temperatures and diminishes as the temperature approaches $T_c$. It is expected that order parameter fluctuations peak near $T_c$\cite{curtisProbingBerezinskiiKosterlitzThoulessVortex2024}, and quasiparticle excitations should reduce with decreasing temperature\cite{dolgirevCharacterizingTwodimensionalSuperconductivity2022b}. However, the Hahn-echo $T_2$ sequence is primarily sensitive to frequencies in the megahertz range, unlike the typically investigated $T_1$. Then, it is likely that the dominant factor limiting $T_2$ is the thermally activated motion of the vortex glass. The vortex motion itself reduce as the temperature lowers, showing also the transition from vortex liquid to glass state.

Instead, this noise is driven by the temperature dependence of the penetration depth, $\lambda$. Although vortex motion slows down at lower temperatures, $\lambda$ decreases, causing the magnetic field distribution around individual vortices to become more localized. Consequently, the local magnetic field at the NV center becomes increasingly sensitive to even small changes in vortex position. This is validated using a Langevin equation, as a simple model for vortex motion at finite temperature. The resulting magnetic field fluctuations at the NV center is computed to obtain a temperature dependent decoherence rate, show in \cref{fig:noiseTemp}\cite{jayaramProbingVortexDynamics2025}. This simple model successfully reproduces the inverse temperature dependence observed in the experimental data, confirming the origin of the noise.

\subsection*{Conclusion}

Nanoscale spatiotemporal imaging of vortices in few-layer 2D superconductors is developed, enabling direct visualization of vortex phenomena previously accessible only through indirect measurement signatures. Geometry and 2D superconductor specific vortex arrangement, deformed vortices, direct evidence of vortex melting and the vortex glass and liquid phase, where the configuration is highly cooling-rate dependent are imaged. This reflects the interplay of thermal fluctuations and pinning sites. 

The dynamics of the superconductor spatially and over temperature is investigated, revealing unexpected noise. Both the spatial arrangement and dynamic behavior of vortices at low magnetic fields have significant implications for the collective macroscopic response of the system, as measured by transport, nuclear magnetic resonance, and other indirect techniques. This work establishes direct imaging of superconductors, particularly with SQM to be an essential tool for the study of solid state physics.

\section{Step junctions in 2D superconductors}

The study of 2D superconductors frequently encounters structural inhomogeneities inherent to the mechanical exfoliation process. This is detailed in the previous section. These inhomogeneities are largely ignored for investigator using non-local and spatially averaging probes, but it can be seen that the local arrangement of vortices and the superconducting order parameter is strong influenced by the inhomogeneities and finite spatial extent of the flake\cite{jayaramProbingVortexDynamics2025}.  Unlike epitaxial films, exfoliated flakes often present a terraced morphology characterized by step junctions. These junctions are interfaces where the layer count, and thus the physical thickness, changes abruptly over a sub-nanometer vertical scale\cite{fridmanAnomalousThicknessDependence2025}. These junctions pose a challenge for non-local probes as the measured properties have their organ over two sample thickness and their transition region. Nevertheless, with spatial resolution these junctions provide a unique experimental platform. They allow for the simultaneous interrogation of two distinct superconducting regions within a single exfoliated crystal, facilitating a direct comparison of their magnetic and electronic responses under identical conditions. The transition region between the two regions can also present unique phenomena. 

In 2D superconductors where the film thickness $d$ is significantly smaller than the bulk London penetration depth $\lambda$, magnetic field screening becomes markedly less efficient. In this regime, the characteristic length scale for magnetic field distribution is governed by the Pearl length $\Lambda = \frac{2\lambda^2}{d}$. The Pearl length is inversely proportional to the film thickness, meaning that variations in the number of atomic layers result in changes to the vortex magnetic profile. Consequently, a step junction represents a spatial discontinuity in the superfluid density. SQM imaging reveals that vortices in thinner regions exhibit broader, more diffuse magnetic field distributions compared to those in thicker terraces, where screening is more robust\cite{jayaramProbingVortexDynamics2025,fridmanAnomalousThicknessDependence2025}. 

Step junctions act as geometric barriers or pinning centers for magnetic vortices. As a vortex approaches a step, it encounters a gradient in its self-energy due to the change in $\Lambda$ and the associated magnetic flux distribution\cite{agassiThicknessdependentPinningSuperconductor2007}. This interaction can lead to the accumulation of vortices along the step edge, where the energetic cost of crossing the thickness discontinuity is maximized. Furthermore, the asymmetry at these junctions can induce non-reciprocal vortex motion, a phenomenon recently identified as the superconducting vortex diode effect\cite{gutfreundDirectObservationSuperconducting2023}. This asymmetry could alter the reciprocity of the critical current required to depin a vortex and become dependent on the direction of the Lorentz force relative to the step geometry\cite{suriNonreciprocityVortexlimitedCritical2022,leeReducingVortexDensity1999}.

At the interface of a step junction, the superconducting wavefunction from the adjacent regions must satisfy boundary conditions that enforce phase coherence. If the transition between thicknesses is sufficiently sharp, the junction may behave as a weak link, hosting Josephson-like dynamics\cite{yabukiSupercurrentVanWaals2016}. Magnetic imaging provides insights into the proximity effects between layers of varying thickness at and away from the junctions. SQM provides an excellent tool to map the static and dynamic magnetic signatures with high spatial resolution and over a wide range of temperatures and magnetic fields. The probe is not affected by large step sizes or residue and bubbles that are a consequence of exfoliation processes.

\subsection{SQM imaging of a step junction in \ce{NbSe2}}

Steps junctions in \ce{NbSe2} are created during the exfoliation process, and they typically exhibit a step height of a few \unit{\nano\meter}, corresponding to a few layers. The step junction is initially identified using a confocal scan of the sample. The optical microscope image of the sample can identify under DIC contrast the difference in layer count. Identifying markers such as bubbles and residue is used to compare with the confocal image, since these markers often show as bright features. Once the step edge is located the, the SQM scan is performed. An SQM scan on relatively thick \ce{NbSe2} flake is shown in \cref{fig:step_s6}. A slow field cooling procedure from above $T_c$ is performed to ensure a relaxed vortex lattice and reproducible measurement conditions. The temperature is also set to be above the base temperature of the measurement cryostat to ensure sufficient cooling power to the sample and probe, and therefore a reliable and accurate temperature throughout the measurement.

The topography SQM image shows a few features, although the height resolution and noise is quite high due to some difficulty in maintaining tip sample contact. To the top left, the \ce{NbSe2}-\ce{hBN} step edge is visible, allowing the confirmation of the meissner effect of the superconductor. From the left to the right of the flake a very faint step edge is visible. This step edge corresponds to a height difference of $\sim\qty{0.7}{\nano\meter}$ or one layer. There is also a small bubble visible in the topography, which can be used for repeated identification of the region. The SQM magnetometry reveals many interesting details. 

The two thickness regions are clearly visible in their different partial screening of the bias magnetic field. The thinner region to the right of the flake exhibits weaker screening of the field visible as reduced intensity of the blue (negative) regions. The vortices also appear to be more irregular and merging into each other as compared to the other region. Nevertheless, due to the relatively large thickness ($\sim\qty{30}{\nano\meter}$) of the entire flake the vortex arrangement is more uniform than the previously investigated samples, which only a few layers. 

Approaching the thicker region to the left, a seemingly merged line of vortices is visible. These vortices appear to be elongated, and merging into one another in the direction defined by the step edge. The accumulation is seemingly only on the thinner flake side. On thicker side, the Meissner effect at the boundaries shows interesting behavior. The width of the boundary Meissner region at the step edge is $\sim\qty{100}{\nano\meter}$ more as compared to the sample edge. The field repulsion is also stronger at the step edge, showing a field reduction of $\sim\qty{30}{\micro\tesla}$ more than the sample edge. These render the  edge's magnetic signature significantly more visible than in the topography. 

To the left of the edge, expected behavior is visible again. The partial Meissner screening is stronger on the thicker region and a more negative magnetic field value is seen, with the vortices seemingly better separated. The vortices still exhibit irregular shapes and arrangement. 

\subsection*{Magnetic field dependence - step junction in thin \ce{NbSe2}}

The difference in the magnetic field response between thin and thick \ce{NbSe2} can be strong (see \cref{fig:thick_thin}). The vortices appear in a more irregular pattern and the Meissner response is weaker. The magnetic signature of such a flake along with a step edge is interesting. \cref{fig:step_fields} show the SQM magnetometry of a flake of thickness $\qty{12.55}{\nano\meter}$ corresponding to 17 layers in the thick region and $\qty{5.03}{\nano\meter}$ corresponding to 5 layers in the thin region, the same sample as investigated in \cref{fig:vortices_s2}. 

The measurements here are performed under slow field cooling to \qty{3.1}{\kelvin} at various bias magnetic fields. The number of vortices increase with increasing applied magnetic field, as is expected. The other observation is the increased measurement noise in the magnetometry. Here, and spatial smoothening is avoided deliberately, and the raw data presented for two reasons. One, the step junction shows a significant merging of vortices and this effect can be misrepresented if smoothened using a filter to reduce noise. Second, it is important to note that \ce{NbSe2}, especially at the few layer limit, exhibit quenching of the NV center contrast when in contact, therefore increasing the measurement noise due to a reduced SNR. This effect is not well understood and requires further investigation. Some preliminary measurements attempting to understand this is presented in \cref{app:contrast}. 

For all applied magnetic fields in \cref{fig:step_fields}, the Meissner repulsion is stronger on the thicker side, with more distinctly separated vortices. The accumulation of vortices at the step edge is also present. Even at lower fields with low vortex density, as in \cref{fig:step_fields_a}, with an applied magnetic field of \qty{24}{\milli\tesla}, the vortices are seemingly elongated and merging. The effect is enhanced or simply more visible at higher vortex densities. 

One possible explanation is supported by the previously described motion vortices, and the time averaged nature of the SQM scan. Since the vortices are understood to be moving, the step edge could act as a site where the motion is enhanced along the direction of the step edge. This effect as seen by an averaging sensor, could appear as an elongated vortex. 

At the maximum applied field of \qty{200}{\milli\tesla} in \cref{fig:step_fields_d}, the number of irregular appearing vortices is significant. At the step edge, individual and vortex shaped features are not visible anymore.

Besides the shape of these vortices, it is noted that the magnetic field measured in the vortex region is not uniform. This directly relates to the enclosed magnetic flux quantum. The fundamental property of a superconductor is the quantization of vorticity\cite{onsagerStatisticalHydrodynamics1949,londonProblemMolecularTheory1948,abrikosovMagneticPropertiesSuperconductors1957}, or the magnetic flux of these vortices. The magnetic flux $\Phi$ is defined as the integral of the magnetic field $\mathbf{B}$ over the sample, i.e., $\Phi = \int dxdy \mathbf{B} \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}$, for the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ normal to the sample surface. The implication is the quantization of magnetic field penetrating a superconductor, i.e., the magnetic field in a type-II superconductor increases in steps of the magnetic flux quanta $\Phi_0 = hc/2e$ (CGS units)\cite{abrikosovMagneticPropertiesSuperconductors1957}. These vortices also show a phase change of $2\pi$ of the complex superconducting order parameter around vortex core. This quantization holds true for the variety of superconducting materials with integer flux quanta, irrespective of other superconducting properties, material, composition, temperature, purity, etc.\cite{iguchiSuperconductingVorticesCarrying2023}. This is supported by many experimental works\cite{changScanningHallProbe1992,hessScanningtunnelingmicroscopyStudyDistortion1992,schlusselWideFieldImagingSuperconductor2018}. Despite the established universality of the integer flux quantization, there are reports where it is possible to have vortex excitations that carry a fraction of the flux quantum. This fraction is not a rational number, and exhibits continuous change dependent on temperature, challenging the universality of the flux quantization\cite{iguchiSuperconductingVorticesCarrying2023}.

The results in \ce{NbSe2} presented here entertain a similar conclusion, calling for a thorough investigation of more sample at various field and temperature conditions. An additional step junction in the vicinity immediately presents more complex behavior (see \cref{fig:fig:large_step_app}). The layered nature of this superconductor further allows for the study of complex junctions at various step sizes and angle, twisting between layers, and heterostructures.
