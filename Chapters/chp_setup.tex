\section{Introduction}

\section{NV microscopy}

At the heart of the SQM system is the NV center, its optical readout and coherent control. To realize this, a confocal microscope paired with microwave (MW) instrumentation is necessary, attached to a cryogenic system. This necessitates additional constraints regarding mounting and size when designing the optical system. Some of the essential components and considerations to make a NV microscopy optical system suitable for use with the AD2200, are explained in detail here.

The AD2200 is equipped with a relatively long ($\sim\qty{1}{\meter}$) sample stick that is inserted in to the center of the cryostat. Due to the requirement of better thermal isolation from ambient conditions, the sample stick (essentially an optical cage system) has its structural rods constructed partially from insulating teflon-like material. This renders very good cryogenic performance but cause difficulty in optical alignment due to its lack of rigidity. The stick is further inserted into  a low clearance tube with the cold gaseous \ce{He} environment. This tube is rigid, and defines then the end configuration of the sample stick. The optics is therefore constructed as a cage system based optical `head' that is permanently mounted to the top of the sample stick.

The optical head is a standalone confocal microscope excitation and collection system requiring only the input of excitation laser with an SM-Fiber and output of collected light via an SM/MM-Fiber. The optical head incorporates the following functions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Collimation and steering of excitation beam
    \item Steering and focussing of collection beam
    \item Polarization control of excitation beam
    \item Imaging of back reflection from sample
    \item Spectral and spatial filtering for confocal microscopy
\end{itemize}

The use of the cage system allows for permanent mounting of the optics head onto the sample stick due to its integrated design and light weight. The lasing and detection system is built on small mobile optical table that allows with only two fiber connections between the optics head and itself. To enable easier handling, the optics head and sample stick (amounting to $\sim\qty{8}{\kilo\gram}$) are inserted/retracted from the cryostat using a crane built onto the cryostat frame. This also ensures that the relatively low rigidity sample stick is not bent and maintains optical alignment. 

\subsection*{Excitation system}

The excitation system is designed as a standalone unit that can be easily separated from the optical head where it is connected to with one single mode fiber. The system itself is built on a mobile optical table, allowing easy access around the cryostat when necessary. This also allows easy alignment of the optical system independent of the optical head.

    \centering 
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/excitation_aom.pdf}
    \caption[Illustration of NV optical excitation system]{Simplified illustration of the excitation system, fiber coupled to the optical head. The labelled components are half-wave plate (HWP), quarter-wave plate (QWP), acousto optical modulator (AOM), polarizing beam splitter (PBS), beam block (BB), and fiber coupler (FC). The excitation begins at the laser module (blue), and passes twice through the AOM, and terminates at the fiber coupler.}

The laser is an `Opus Gem' at \qty{532}{\nano\meter}. The linear polarized beam passes through the HWP, which is rotated for maximum transmission of the beam through the PBS. The beam is focused to a spot on the AOM (3350-199, Gooch \& Housego), corresponding to the AOM crystal size specification. This ensures a rise/fall time of the modulated beam in specification, and a long lifetime of the AOM. The beams (0 and 1\textsuperscript{st} order) are collimated, and the 0 order beam is blocked. The 1\textsuperscript{st} order beam passes through a QWP twice, accumulating a rotation of \ang{180}, and passes back through the same path, through the AOM. The beam deflection acquired during the first pass is reversed during this second pass. This configuration is called the double-pass AOM configuration\cite{donleyDoublepassAcoustoopticModulator2005}. The beam is then reflected from the PBS due to the accumulated phase, and is coupled into a fiber. 

The advantage of using an AOM is a high extinction ratio and fast switching of the laser. This AOM driver also allows for easy control of the laser power with an analog voltage input, which allows for pulse sequences that require control of excitation power\cite{wirtitschExploitingIonizationDynamics2023b} and relative control of power between different types of measurements.

\subsection*{Optical head}

The optical head is the next system in line that is developed for the NV microscope. It is designed to be compact, easy to align and modify, and allows for complete control of the optics. It can also so be further extended to other wavelengths to allow for spin-to-charge conversion readout\cite{shieldsEfficientReadoutSingle2015}. The cage system construction also makes it an easy to remove single unit, with the possibility of adding in drop-in optical elements. A simplified illustration of the optical head is shown in \cref{fig:opt_head}.

A description of the components of the optical head is as follows: the single mode (SM) polarization maintaining fiber from the excitation system is coupled to the optical head at the top. The collimation of the laser is done with an Olympus \qty{0.25}{NA} objective. The polarization is rotated with a HWP mounted on a motorized rotation stage. The laser is cleaned up with a \qty{532}{\nano\meter} band pass (BP) filter. The beam is directed downwards with two angled mirrors, and passes through a 90:10 beam splitter (BS), and a short pass dichroic mirror (DM). Two angled mirrors further align the beam to the high-NA low temperature objective at the end of the sample stick. The fluorescence collected by the objective is directed by the last two angled mirrors onto the DM, where it is reflected onto the collection path. The shorter wavelengths are directed onto the back reflection imaging camera. The collection path has a long pass (LP) \qty{650}{\nano\meter} filter, and an aspheric lens to focus the fluorescence on a multi-mode (MM) fiber.

The construction allows for the permanent mounting of the optics head to the top of the sample stick, bringing the realignment time close to zero. Due to the rigid construction of the cage system and the use of flexure based linear stages wherever possible, the long term stability of the optics is also very suitable for SQM measurement that can often take weeks for large phase space sweeps.

The optics head achieves high counts from commercially available SQM tips. \cref{fig:confocal} shows an exemplary confocal scan take at $\sim\qty{100}{\micro\watt}$ of excitation power. The NV center is the fluorescent bright spot roughly at the center of the tip. The spatial profile of this spot is also indicative the well-designed and aligned confocal optics, owing to the gaussian like emission and good background suppression. The outline of the tip can be identified by scattering that occurs at the boundary. \subsection*{Spin control}

\section{AFM modalities}

The technique that enables the more accessible use of NV centers in diamond as a high spatial resolution measurement device is atomic force microscopy. Hence, it is important to understand and establish the correct method for its use, especially at low temperatures, vacuum environment and with expensive, fragile probes. 

\subsection*{Control loops}
\phantomsection
\label{sub:control_loops}

AFM is a technique built on feedback loops. A setpoint quantity is observed, and an output changed based on a deviation from this setpoint, such that the setpoint is achieved again. This setpoint can be various quantities such as the intensity of laser beam reflected off the end of a probe, or the frequency of oscillation of such an intensity. SQM tips that are commercially available are built on top of a self-sensing and actuating probe system called the Akiyama probe\cite{akiyamaImplementationCharacterizationQuartz2010}. The Akiyama probe is an excellent host for an NV probe due to the AFM sensing being optics free. It uses instead, the charge build-up across a piezo-electric tuning fork that the sharp probe is attached to. This tuning fork is oscillated, either by electrically driving it or mechanically by a nearby oscillating piezo-element. In this system, mechanical driving is utilized, due to both simplicity of the system and to avoid potential cross talk that can occur with other lines. The charge build-up across the tuning fork is picked up by contacts connected to a charge amplifier, which gives a voltage signal that is read out. This voltage signal oscillates at the frequency of the tuning fork. The resonance frequency of one of the eigen-modes the combined tuning fork and probe assembly (Akiyama probe) is determined and driven.

The oscillation of the Akiyama probe offers the various setpoint quantities that is maintained for its operation as an AFM probe. The oscillation amplitude ($HF_{amp}$), frequency and phase ($HF_{phase}$) of the high frequency (HF) oscillation with respect to the driving signal can act as a setpoint. In this system, the primarily utilized control loop utilizes a combination of the $HF_{amp}$, and a phase locked loop (PLL) of the $HF_{phase}$. First, a frequency sweep is performed to find the resonance of the tip, typically around \qty{32}{\kilo\hertz} for Akiyama like probes. The excitation amplitude ($A_{exc}$) and detection voltage range (determines the effective gain and voltage resolution of the HF amplitude) is set such that there is good SNR and true oscillation displacement is on the order of a few $\unit{\nano\meter}$ (determined via optical or scanning means). Typical values are $A_{exc}=\qty{100}{\milli\volt}$, $HF_{amp}=\qty{250}{\milli\volt}$, detection voltage range around \qty{1}{\volt}.

The $HF_{phase}$ is set as zero at resonance condition. A PLL loop is activated with the $HF_{phase}=0$ as the setpoint with the PLL control variable being $dF$, the change in driving frequency from. The maximum range in $dF$ is set and noted (a good range is $\pm \qty{20}{\hertz}$), since this defines the available spectral resolution of the PLL loop. The PID loop parameters are tuned for the PLL loop to achieve an $HF_{phase}$ that closely follows $dF$. 

The setpoint for the AFM is the z-control loop setpoint, which is set to the $HF_{amp}$ variable and typically to around 80\% of the free oscillation amplitude. Once contact is achieved, the PID parameters are also tuned to for the z-output to closely follow the $HF_{amp}$ setpoint.

A common issue for low temperature operation with a control loop dependent on a mechanical oscillator is the very large  Q factors (>5000) that occur due to the temperature and atmosphere. Small perturbations to the oscillator, such as AFM contact, can cause a very large change in the $HF_{amp}$, making the control loop unstable for achievable PID parameters. The result is extremely slow AFM scans or in extreme cases makes AFM contact not possible. This challenge can be largely mitigated by effective understanding and use of Q control\cite{fairbairnResonantControlAtomic2012}, which is possible in the system's AFM controller (ASC500, Attocube/N-Hands).

\subsection*{Q control}
An exemplary control loop used to reduce the effective Q factor of the probe is shown in \cref{fig:qcontrol_block}. It makes use of a negative feedback loop of the probe's velocity. This velocity can simply be estimated from the displacement amplitude signal. For commercial AFMs and likely the control implemented in this system, the feedback for a displacement $s$ is defined as
\begin{equation}
    \centering 
    K(s)=Ge^{-T_d s},
\end{equation}
where $T_d$ is a time delay and $G$ the gain\cite{fairbairnResonantControlAtomic2012}. 

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/qcontrol_block.pdf}
    \caption[Block diagram of Q control loop]{Block diagram illustrating Q control. Reused with permission from AIP Publishing\cite{fairbairnResonantControlAtomic2012}.}
    \label{fig:qcontrol_block}
\end{figure}

The implementation in this system requires a similar input of `phase' in degrees and `feedback' of arbitrary units. A value between 0 and 1 is allowed as feedback with 1 giving complete cancellation of $HF_{amp}$. The other important parameter for effective control of the Q factor is $A_{exc}$. Frequency sweeps are performed to estimate the phase for which the Q factor is at minimum. This phase need not be adjusted for further tuning of the Q factor since it is independent of the other two variables. The $A_{exc}$ parameter is now used to tune the Q factor to a desired value. Up to one order of magnitude reduction of the Q factor is possible. The feedback parameter is only used to achieve control over the final $HF_{amp}$, and does not directly affect the Q factor. It is noted that the loop becomes ineffective at high $A_{exc}$ and cannot be used to further reduce the Q factor.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/qcontrol.pdf}
    \caption[Resonance curves under Q control]{Resonance curves measured with various Q control parameters. Increasing $A_{exc}$ and feedback parameter decreases the Q factor while maintaining the $HF_{amp}$.}
    \label{fig:qcontrol}
\end{figure}

\cref{fig:qcontrol} shows resonance curves obtained by implementing the above protocol. The phase parameter is fixed at \ang{160}. For ($A_{exc}$, feedback) values of (\qty{50}{\milli\volt}, 0.591), (\qty{1}{\volt}, 0.943), and (\qty{4}{\volt}, 0.956), Q factors of 2233, 1053, and 498 respectively are obtained. With these lower Q factors along with the previously described control loops, the probe achieves good AFM contact and scans even at low temperatures and \ce{He} environment. This protocol is also extended to use in a room temperature SQM system and works similarly well, although the native Q factors are not high.

\section{SQM modalities}

The simultaneous operation of NV microscopy and AFM enables SQM. The previously described sections establish the necessary elements for spin spate readout of an NV center at in an AFM probe, while performing a scan across a sample of interest. The AFM scan is performed mainly in two ways: lift-off and contact. 

In contact mode tip continuously maintains contact with the sample while the measurement takes place. The stand-off of the NV center depends on the depth of the NV in the diamond, and the setpoint of the AFM contact\cite{xuMinimizingSensorSampleDistances2025}. 

To measure stand-off dependant magnetic properties or to protect the NV from strong sample induced decoherence, the lift-off mode is used instead. Here, the topography is measured at the current position before retracting the sample by a set amount. The measurement is then done with the sample retracted away (typically \qty{20}{\nano\meter}), the z-control loop is reactivated, and the sample is in contact again, proceeding to the next position.

In either of these contact modes, magnetometry is done in multiple ways. CW-ODMR is the basic measurement that provides information on the magnetic fields present on the sample\cite{barrySensitivityOptimizationNVdiamond2020}. This measurement although suffers from lower sensitivity due to partial polarization, no decoupling between spin initialization and polarization mechanisms, and often heating of the sample and surroundings which is critical in low temperature operations, sometimes with low cooling power. To mitigate this pulsed laser and MW measurements are performed, which increases sensitivity and allows for spectrally tailored sensitivity/decoupling.

PODMR provides maximal contrast similar to Ramsey like measurement and unlike CW-ODMR, while avoiding power broadening due to laser and MW, approaching $T_2^*$ sensitivity\cite{dreauAvoidingPowerBroadening2011,barrySensitivityOptimizationNVdiamond2020}. A scan employing PODMR although is sensitive to spatial and temporal changes in Rabi frequency, but this typically not the case for samples investigated in this system as well for small scan ranges with the MW antenna nearby. 

For high spatial resolution scans, a measurement time speedup is desirable. Modest reductions in measurement time is made possible by employing smart Bayesian sampling of measurement points, such that the measurements time/frequency positions are alloted such that a cost function corresponding to information regarding the ODMR spectrum is minimized\cite{dushenkoSequentialBayesianExperiment2020}. 

On the other hand, a complete PODMR measurement contains multiple points of information (number of peaks, contrast, line center, shape, symmetry), all of which is not necessary for every measurement, to map the magnetic field of a sample. Much of the measurement time is spent collecting the lineshape, which, under the assumption that it stays constant over the sample and for the duration of the measurement, can be disregarded. This assumption holds true for many samples. Even under the change of the linewidth, the line center can be estimated only with the measurement of two points, as long the change of line center is not more than the linewidth over the course of the consecutive measurements. A demonstration of such a measurement with lock-in detection, allowing for real time magnetic field detection is shown by \textcite{schoenfeldRealTimeMagnetic2011}. An adaptation of this lock-in method for PODMR is used in this system to achieve fast measurement times.

\subsection*{Two-point tracking}

The two-point tracking mode, as the name suggest, reduces the measurement to only two frequency points. The frequency points to measure, $f_{\pm}$, are chosen on either side of the last measured resonance, such that $f_{\pm}=f_0\pm\Delta$, for a resonance frequency $f_0$. The $\Delta$ is chosen at the first spatial scan point, based on a measured PODMR spectrum that captures the complete line shape of the resonance. This is done manually by determining the range of linear change in the slope of the measured Lorentzian, or by setting it to be at the points of maximum inflection. The slope, $s$, of the linear part around the resonance in the first derivative of the measured Lorentzian is also calculated. 

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/tracking.pdf}
    \caption[ODMR with tracking mode]{Illustration of the two-point tracking mode. Black ODMR spectrum is under resonance condition, and the red spectrum is off resonant by $\delta\nu$. }
    \label{fig:tracking}
\end{figure}

The implementation of pulsed readout with changing MW frequency also helps make this a fast measurement with very little hardware based delays that can slow the measurement down. The MW/local oscillator (LO) source maintains a fixed output frequency which then is modulated by the combination of an IQ mixer (built into the LO), and an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG). This allows the fast switching of frequency by playing the corresponding modulating waveform on the AWG. A pulsed readout is performed with the AWG modulating the fixed LO at $f_0$, setting the output MW to $f_{-}$, and then subsequently for $f_{-}$. This pair of pulsed readout is repeated $N$ times to achieved good SNR.

The necessary quantities to estimate the shift in resonance frequency $\delta_{\nu}$, are counts $c_{\pm}$ at $f_{\pm}$, $s$, and the last known resonance frequency $f_0$, which are measured. The amount of change in counts is proportional to shift in the resonance frequency, as shown in \cref{fig:tracking}. This amount of change in counts is quantified as,

\begin{equation}
    \centering
    visibility = \frac{(c_- - c_+)}{(c_+ + c_-)}.
\end{equation}

The shift in resonance frequency is calculated as,

\begin{equation}
    \centering
    \delta_{\nu} = \frac{visibility}{s}.
\end{equation}

The new $f_0$ is then set to be $f_0 + \delta_{\nu}$, and the measurement is repeated at the next spatial scan point. The estimation can also be repeated multiple times at one position to achieve lower errors, since the range of frequency where the slope is valid is limited by the linewidth of the resonance, albeit at the cost of measurement time.

\subsection*{Gradiometry and lock-in}

A coincidental feature of the SQM is the mechanical oscillation of the probe, which is used to maintain and control AFM contact with the sample. This oscillation is typically in plane for commercially available tips. In conventional SQM modes, this small oscillation is averaged over during the measurement, with little impact on resolution for typical operation. Even in this configuration, state-of-the-art SQMs achieve sensitivities of a few \unit{\micro\tesla/\sqrthertz}\cite{sunMagneticDomainsDomain2021,songDirectVisualizationMagnetic2021}. In probes on an oscillating tip such as SQUID-on-tip (SOT), increased sensitivity has been demonstrated by utilizing the mechanical oscillation convert a static gradient signal into an effective time varying signal, as seen by the sensor. This technique of 'gradiometry' is demonstrated by \textcite{huxterScanningGradiometrySingle2022} resolving magnetic stray fields with a sensitivity of $\sim\qty{120}{\nano\tesla/\sqrthertz}$. To improve the resolution, sensitivity and to make the mechanical oscillation of the probe accessible for future measurements, gradiometry is implemented in the RT SQM system. An overview of the technique and results with gradiometry is presented in this section.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/gradiometry.pdf}
    \caption[Illustration of gradiometry]{Illustration of gradiometry. The variables in red are used to maximize the collected phase. The AWG requires a fixed time $t_s$ until the first sample is played from the recognition of the trigger, which is accounted for in $t_0$.}
    \label{fig:gradiometry}
\end{figure}

The principle of gradiometry is illustrated in \cref{fig:gradiometry}. The tip oscillates in a coherent manner over a sample with a static gradient of magnetic field; the same principles also largely apply for a sample with a driven oscillating magnetic field, but the description here is restricted assuming the former case. The mechanical oscillation is driven by the SPM controller that sends a high frequency (HF) driving signal to a piezo element attached near the probe. This is the actuator for the mechanical oscillations. A reference signal of the same frequency and phase, HF sync., is sent to the trigger inputs of the AWG. This allows the AWG to trigger pulse sequences synchronized with the mechanical oscillations of the probe. It is also possible to collect the readout signal of the true probe oscillation, instead of the driving signal, but this being a noisy signal is difficult to trigger on. The driving signal, along with the active PLL loop (page \pageref{sub:control_loops}), enables precise triggering of the AWG. This is also confirmed to be operational at cryogenic conditions. Nevertheless, gradiometry is performed at lift-off mode since the contact operation can induce strain driven fluctuations in the NV\cite{huxterScanningGradiometrySingle2022,ovartchaiyapongDynamicStrainmediatedCoupling2014} or introduces additional mechanical oscillation modes. The mechanical oscillation of the probe is described by position of the probe $x(t)=x_0 + x_{osc} \sin(2\pi f_{TF}t)$, for an oscillation amplitude $x_{osc}\sim\qty{10}{\nano\meter}$, and $f_{TF}\sim\qtylist{32;195}{\kilo\hertz}$.

The stray field experienced by the NV center is given by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        B(x(t)) &= B(x_0) + \left. \frac{\partial B}{\partial x} \right\rvert_{x=x_0} x_{osc} \sin(2\pi f_{TF}t) \\
        &\quad + \frac{1}{2} \left. \frac{\partial^2 B}{\partial x^2} \right\rvert_{x=x_0} x^2_{osc} \sin^2(2\pi f_{TF}t) + \ldots,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where B is the magnetic field along the NV axis.
The significant amplitudes corresponding to $0f_{TF}$, $1f_{TF}$, and $2f_{TF}$ are given by
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        B_0 &= B(x_0) \\
        B_1 &= x_{osc} \left. \frac{\partial B}{\partial x} \right\rvert_{x=x_0} \\
        B_2 &= \frac{1}{2} x^2_{osc} \left. \frac{\partial^2 B}{\partial x^2} \right\rvert_{x=x_0} 
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
proportional to the static, gradient and second derivative of the magnetic field, respectively\cite{huxterScanningGradiometrySingle2022}.
Experimentally, this is realized by measuring the quantum phase accumulated during an AC magnetic field sensing sequence that is synchronized with the mechanical oscillations of the probe. The $n\text-f_{TF}$ harmonic is measured by CPMG-$n$ (Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill) sequences having $n$ spin inverting $\pi-$pulses during the interrogation time\cite{degenQuantumSensing2017}. For $1f_{TF}$, the Hahn-echo sequence is identical to CPMG-1 (except for the phase of the inverting pulse). The pulse sequence is shown in \cref{fig:gradiometry}. The phase (azimuthal angle of the state vector in the equatorial plane) accumulated for CPMG-1 corresponding to the first harmonic or $B_1$ is given by

\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \phi &= \int_{t=t_0-\tau/2}^{t_0} \gamma_e B(t)dt - \int_{t_0}^{t=t_0+\tau/2} \gamma_e B(t)dt \\
            &= -\gamma_e B_1 \frac{2 sin^2(\pi f_{TF} \tau/2 )}{\pi f_{TF}},
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

when the pulse sequence is centered with respect to the tuning fork oscillation. This phase can be calculated using a four-phase readout. In the CPMG-1 sequence, the final $(\pi/2)_\Phi$ has $\Phi = X,-X,Y-Y$ (orthogonal axes), corresponding to a fluorescence of $C_{X,-X,Y,-Y}$ on readout. 

Let the state before the final pulse be $|\psi_{eq}\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} \left( |0\rangle + e^{i\phi} |1\rangle \right)$. A $\pm X$ pulse rotates the $y$ component to the $\pm z$ axis. The projection is $\pm \sin\phi$. A $\pm Y$ pulse rotates the $x$-component to the $\mp z$-axis. The projection is $\mp \cos\phi$. Taking the ratio of the differential signals yields the tangent of the phase:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:four_phase}
    \begin{split}
        \frac{C_{X}-C_{-X}}{C_{-Y}-C_{Y}} = \frac{2\sin\phi}{2\cos\phi} = \tan\phi \\
        \phi = \text{arctan}\left (\frac{C_{X}-C_{-X}}{C_{-Y}-C_{Y}} \right ).
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

This four-phase readout is represented on the Bloch sphere in \cref{fig:bloch_grad}. The spin is initialized in state $|0\rangle$, and experiences a small time varying detuning, $B_{ac}$, field along $z$. A Hahn-Echo pulse sequence is performed, synchronized with the detuning field, as in the ideal gradiometry case. Before the final $(\pi/2)_\Phi$ pulse, the state vector points at the depicted red star. From this point on, the state is rotated around the $\pm y$ axis (blue), or the $\pm x$ axis (red). The Bloch sphere on the right shows the final state vectors and their corresponding $z$ projections ($C_{X,-X,Y,-Y}$). The calculated phase $\phi$ and the corresponding vector is shown in black. The measured phase is in the range $[-\pi, \pi)$\cite{palmImagingSubmicroampereCurrents2022} and requires phase unwrapping for signals outside of this range. The amplitude of $\phi$ is determined by the strength of gradient field, interrogation time $\tau$, and oscillation amplitude $x_{osc}$.

\begin{figure}[h]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{../figures/grad_bloch.pdf}
    \caption[Four phase readout for gradiometry]{Four phase readout for gradiometry. The Bloch sphere on the left shows a $|0\rangle$ initialized state evolving under an AC detuning and Hahn-Echo sequence, with a final $(\pi/2)_\Phi$ pulse. The two accumulated phases of $\phi/2$ are shown. The Bloch sphere on the right shows the $z$ projections of the final sates and the calculated phase $\phi$.}

As a first step in performing gradiometry measurements, the synchronization of the tip oscillation and spin readout is established. The synchronization time $t_0$ determines the arrival of any sequence after the trigger by the mechanical oscillation. This is depicted in \cref{fig:gradiometry}. To verify correct synchronization, the fluorescence is measured as a function of the $t_0$ time. The fluorescence from a short \qty{200}{\nano\second} window is integrated from the total \qty{3}{\micro\second} readout pulse. The probe is oscillating at a fixed frequency of $\sim\qty{32}{\kilo\hertz}$. For a fixed excitation beam and an emitter oscillating in and out of the beam, a periodic fluorescence signal is expected. The measurement for various probe excitation amplitudes $A_{exc}$ is shown in \cref{fig:pl_osc}. The oscillation is fit to a function for an oscillating emitter in a Gaussian beam (see \cref{app:pl_osc}). The extracted oscillation amplitudes from the fit are shown in the figure, showing a maximum of $\qty{30(2)}{\nano\meter}$. The oscillation amplitudes can also be extracted by performing time resolved and locked in readout while doing an AFM scan over a magnetic/fluorescence quenching feature of known dimensions\cite{huxterScanningGradiometrySingle2022}.

With the synchronization between mechanical oscillation of the probe and control pulse sequence established, the gradiometry phase can be measured. To this end, the probe is approached with a current carrying wire that can be easily localized and the {{\O}}rsted field controlled with the applied voltage. This also allows for testing on the same sample a lock-in measurement, which is simply gradiometry but with synchronization to the applied voltage instead.

A conventional magnetometry scan is performed to localize the probe over a region with strong magnetic gradient. The measurements are then done in lift-off mode. A conventional CPMG-1 sequence is performed and the $T_2$ time estimated. This is used to define a reasonable interrogation time $\tau$ for the gradiometry sequence. It is assumed that the voltage trigger levels are set such that successful triggering takes place. These trigger values are optimized as well, to minimize time $t_0$ such that measurement dead time is reduced (see \cref{app:phase_volt}).

The phase measurement with a sweep over $t_0$ is performed to determine the shortest $t_0$ value that gives a strong phase signal. This indicates that the CPMG-1 sequence is performed centered with the mechanical oscillation and therefore centered with the pseudo-oscillating magnetic field. Such a measurement is shown in \cref{fig:phase_osc}. The CPMG-1 sequence with the four-phase readout is loaded onto the AWG as four sequences to be individually triggered. The phase is calculated as in \cref{eq:four_phase}.\footnotemark Note that this measurement is performed for a higher resonance mode of $\sim\qty{195}{\kilo\hertz}$. Here the ideal $t_0$ time is found to be \qty{3}{\micro\second}. 
\footnotetext{The fluorescence signal is calculated only with a summation method (see \cref{app:pl_osc}).}

The $t_0$ is set to the determined value and a spatial scan of the phase is performed. The measurement is performed at a mechanical oscillation frequency of $\sim\qty{195}{\kilo\hertz}$, voltage of \qty{250}{\milli\volt} with a NV probe showing $T_2=\qty{3.4(5)}{\micro\second}$. The gradiometric phase is then collected at every point in a lift-off manner, along with the topography. The measurement is shown in \cref{fig:grad_xy}. The gradiometry scan achieves high sensitivity, rejection of low frequency noise, and captures the expected gradient of the magnetic field. The voltage across the wire can be reduced to \qty{1}{\milli\volt}, such that a signal is not captured anymore by a conventional fast scan. A weak signal of amplitude $\sim B_1=\qty{0.5}{\micro\tesla}$ is still persistent in the gradiometry scan (see \cref{app:grad_xy}).

This technique is also extended to a lock-in like scheme where the current is oscillated instead at a fixed frequency and the spin control is synchronized with this (see \cref{app:lockin_xy}). This measurement then directly measures the $B_0$ field with high sensitivity\cite{palmImagingSubmicroampereCurrents2022}.
